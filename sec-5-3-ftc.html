    Section 5.3 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

    Today’s Question.

    Two different jobs — differentiating and accumulating — keep landing on the same answers. Is that a coincidence?
    🔗
    🔗
    Pancake has been rolling paint onto walls since Section 5.1, and measuring the area \(\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) since Section 5.2. Today she notices something her coach cannot ignore: every time she works out how fast the painted area is growing, she lands right back on the roller’s speed \(f\text{.}\) That is either an enormous coincidence, or it is a theorem.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.1 Pancake’s conjecture

    Remember the two walls. On Wall 1, the roller moves at a constant speed \(f(x) = 3\text{,}\) and the painted area after \(x\) feet is \(A(x) = 3x\text{.}\) On Wall 2, the roller speeds up as it goes, \(f(x) = 2x\text{,}\) and the painted area is \(A(x) = x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now differentiate each area function.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.1. Differentiate the area functions.

    🔗
    Both times, differentiating the accumulated area handed back the roller speed we started with. Pancake’s conjecture:
    🔗

    Pancake’s conjecture.

    If \(A(x)\) is the area painted up to position \(x\text{,}\) then \(A'(x) = f(x)\text{.}\) Accumulating and then differentiating undoes itself.
    🔗
    🔗
    Two examples are not a proof. We need to see why this has to be true for any reasonable \(f\text{,}\) not just these two.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.2 Look at one tiny piece of new area

    Let \(A(x)\) be the area accumulated from \(a\) up to \(x\text{,}\) for some fixed starting point \(a\text{.}\) Now step forward by a small amount \(\Delta x\text{.}\) The new area \(A(x + \Delta x)\) is the old area \(A(x)\) plus one thin strip: a sliver running from \(x\) to \(x + \Delta x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    When \(\Delta x\) is small, that sliver is almost a rectangle: its width is \(\Delta x\text{,}\) and its height is approximately \(f(x)\text{,}\) the value of the roller speed right at the left edge of the strip. So
    \begin{equation*} A(x + \Delta x) - A(x) \;\approx\; f(x)\,\Delta x. \end{equation*}
    This is exactly the running-triangle-plus-trapezoid-strip picture from the FTC paint-roller activity: the new paint added in one step is close to (rate) \(\times\) (step size), and the approximation gets better and better as the step shrinks.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.3 Turn the area change into a rate

    Divide both sides of \(A(x+\Delta x) - A(x) \approx f(x)\,\Delta x\) by \(\Delta x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{A(x+\Delta x) - A(x)}{\Delta x} \;\approx\; f(x). \end{equation*}
    That left-hand side should look familiar — it is exactly the difference quotient from the definition of the derivative. Let \(\Delta x \to 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A'(x) \;=\; \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{A(x+\Delta x) - A(x)}{\Delta x} \;=\; f(x). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This is exactly the slider demonstration from the Doenet pre-assignment: on Wall 1 the rate \(\Delta A/\Delta x\) sat still at \(3\) no matter the step size, while on Wall 2 the rate only settled on \(f(x) = 2x\) once \(\Delta x\) was squeezed down toward \(0\text{.}\) Squeezing \(\Delta x \to 0\) is precisely what turns an approximation into an exact derivative.
    🔗

    Fact 5.3.2. Pancake’s conjecture, proved.

    If \(A(x)\) is the accumulated area up to \(x\text{,}\) then \(A'(x) = f(x)\text{.}\) This is no longer a guess — it follows from the definition of the derivative applied to one thin strip.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.4 Write the accumulation function using integral notation

    We already have a name for the accumulated area: it is a definite integral with a variable upper bound,
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt. \end{equation*}
    (The variable inside is renamed \(t\) so it is not confused with the upper bound \(x\text{.}\)) Combining this with Fact 5.3.2 gives the first part of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.3. Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1.

    If \(f\) is continuous and \(A(x) = \displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\left(\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\right) = f(x). \end{equation*}
    In words: differentiating an accumulation function gives back the rate you started with.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.5 What is so “fundamental” about it?

    Derivatives measure instantaneous change. Integrals measure total accumulation. These sound like opposite jobs — one zooms in, the other adds up. The Fundamental Theorem says they are secretly inverse operations: accumulate, then differentiate, and you land right back where you started. It is the calculus version of “undo a sum by subtracting.”
    🔗
    There is a second consequence worth naming right away: since \(A'(x) = f(x)\text{,}\) the accumulation function \(A\) is itself an antiderivative of \(f\text{.}\) That one observation is about to unlock every definite integral we could not evaluate by geometry alone.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.6 Three accumulation functions

    \(A(x) = \displaystyle\int_0^x 4\,dt\text{.}\) A constant-height strip of height \(4\) out to \(x\) has area \(A(x) = 4x\text{,}\) so \(A'(x) = 4\) — exactly \(f(x) = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(A(x) = \displaystyle\int_0^x 2t\,dt\text{.}\) This is the familiar triangle: \(A(x) = x^2\text{,}\) so \(A'(x) = 2x\) — exactly \(f(x) = 2x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now a function with no obvious geometric shortcut: \(A(x) = \displaystyle\int_1^x (t^2+3)\,dt\text{.}\) Nothing about this region is a triangle or a rectangle, so geometry alone will not evaluate it. But FTC Part 1 does not care — it never asked us to compute the integral at all.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.4. Differentiate without integrating.

    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.7 From accumulation to a computation formula

    FTC Part 1 tells us \(A\) is an antiderivative of \(f\text{.}\) But in Section 4.9 we learned that any two antiderivatives of the same \(f\) differ only by a constant. So if \(F\) is any antiderivative we already know how to find (power rule, trig rule, whatever), then
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = F(x) + C \end{equation*}
    for some constant \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We can pin down \(C\) using the one value of \(A\) we already know for free: \(A(a) = \displaystyle\int_a^a f(t)\,dt = 0\text{,}\) since accumulating from \(a\) to \(a\) accumulates nothing. So \(0 = F(a) + C\text{,}\) which means \(C = -F(a)\text{.}\) Substituting back,
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^x f(t)\,dt = A(x) = F(x) - F(a). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Nothing stops us from letting the ending point be any value \(b\) instead of a generic \(x\text{.}\) That gives the computational form of the theorem.
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.5. Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2.

    If \(F\) is any antiderivative of a continuous function \(f\) (that is, \(F' = f\)), then
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This one formula is the reason we spent all of Section 4.9 building up antiderivative rules: every one of those rules now evaluates a definite integral instantly, with no limits and no rectangles.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.8 The evaluation bar

    Writing \(F(b) - F(a)\) over and over gets repetitive, so we abbreviate it with an evaluation bar:
    \begin{equation*} \bigl[F(x)\bigr]_a^b \;:=\; F(b) - F(a). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Worked example: evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 x^2\,dx\text{.}\) An antiderivative of \(x^2\) is \(F(x) = \tfrac13x^3\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^3 x^2\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_1^3 = \frac{27}{3} - \frac13 = 9 - \frac13 = \frac{26}{3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.6. You do: compute with FTC Part 2.

    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.9 Return to the car

    Back in Section 4.9, the car with velocity \(v(t) = 3t^2\) gave displacement \(F(2) - F(0) = 8 - 0 = 8\) using the antiderivative \(F(t) = t^3\text{.}\) Back in Section 5.2, the same car gave \(\int_0^2 3t^2\,dt = 8\) as the limit of Riemann sums squeezing tighter and tighter. Two completely different procedures — one algebraic, one about rectangles — landed on the exact same number.
    🔗
    That was never a coincidence. FTC Part 2 is why it had to come out the same way both times:
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2 3t^2\,dt = \bigl[t^3\bigr]_0^2 = 8 - 0 = 8. \end{equation*}
    The antiderivative method is not a different answer to a different question. It is a shortcut for computing the exact Riemann-sum limit, guaranteed by the Fundamental Theorem.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.10 What happened to the \(+C\text{?}\)

    You might expect \(F(b) - F(a)\) to carry a leftover constant. It does not, and it is worth seeing exactly why.
    🔗
    If we used \(F(x) + C\) instead of \(F(x)\) as our antiderivative,
    \begin{equation*} \bigl(F(b)+C\bigr) - \bigl(F(a)+C\bigr) = F(b) - F(a). \end{equation*}
    The constant appears in both terms and cancels exactly. So it never matters which antiderivative you pick — and there is never any need to write \(+C\) when evaluating a definite integral.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.7. Spot the error.

    A student writes \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 3x^2\,dx = x^3+C\text{.}\) What went wrong?
    🔗
    • A definite integral is a number, not a family of functions — the bounds must be used. The correct computation is \([x^3]_0^2 = 8 - 0 = 8\text{.}\)
    • Right. No bounds were ever plugged in, and \(+C\) never belongs on a definite integral.
    • Nothing — \(x^3+C\) is a correct antiderivative of \(3x^2\text{.}\)
    • It is a correct antiderivative, but the question asked for a definite integral — a single number found by evaluating at the bounds, not a family of functions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.11 Putting it together

    Checkpoint 5.3.8. Conceptual check.

    1. In your own words, what does FTC Part 1 say about the relationship between accumulating and differentiating?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Why is the accumulation function \(A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) itself an antiderivative of \(f\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Why does \(+C\) never appear in the final answer to a definite integral?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Differentiating an accumulation function gives back the exact rate that was being accumulated: \(A'(x) = f(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Because FTC Part 1 says \(A'(x) = f(x)\text{,}\) which is exactly the defining property of an antiderivative.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Because the constant appears in both \(F(b)+C\) and \(F(a)+C\text{,}\) and subtracting cancels it.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.9. Exit ticket, part 1.

    If \(A(x) = \displaystyle\int_1^x (t^3+2)\,dt\text{,}\) then \(A'(x) =\) ?
    🔗
    • \(x^3+2\)
    • Yes — by FTC Part 1, differentiating the accumulation function just hands back the integrand.
    • \(\tfrac14 x^4+2x\)
    • That is an antiderivative of \(t^3+2\text{,}\) which is a different question from \(A'(x)\text{.}\) FTC Part 1 says \(A'(x)\) is just the integrand itself.
    • \(3x^2\)
    • Not quite — that would be the derivative of \(x^3\) alone, but the integrand here is \(t^3+2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3.10. Exit ticket, part 2.

    🔗

    Today’s main idea.

    The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus ties the two halves of this chapter together:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\left(\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\right) = f(x) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \text{ when } F' = f. \end{equation*}
    Differentiating undoes accumulating. And once we know any single antiderivative, every definite integral becomes a two-step subtraction — no limits required.
    🔗
    Next question: when there are no bounds at all, what does \(\int f(x)\,dx\) even mean, and how is it different from everything we just did? That is Indefinite Integrals and the Net Change Theorem.
