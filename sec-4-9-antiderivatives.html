    Section 2.5 Antiderivatives

    Today’s Question.

    If we know how something is changing, can we recover the thing itself?
    🔗
    🔗
    For most of your calculus life, you have traveled in one direction:
    \begin{equation*} \text{function} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{derivative}. \end{equation*}
    Today we turn around:
    \begin{equation*} \text{derivative} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{original function?} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Why would anyone care? Suppose you know a car’s velocity, but you want its position. Or you know how fast water is leaking from a tank, but you want to know how much has been lost.
    🔗
    In both cases, you know a rate of change, and you want the quantity that produced it.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.1 Can we run a derivative backward?

    Start with something familiar. Suppose \(F'(x) = 3x^2\text{.}\) Without any new formula, can you find a function \(F\) that works?
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.1. Find one.

    Which function has derivative \(3x^2\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(6x\)
    • That is the derivative of \(3x^2\text{.}\) We need to go the other way.
    • \(x^3\)
    • Yes. Check it: the derivative of \(x^3\) is \(3x^2\text{.}\)
    • \(3x^3\)
    • Check by differentiating: you get \(9x^2\text{,}\) not \(3x^2\text{.}\)
    • \(\dfrac{x^3}{3}\)
    • Check by differentiating: you get \(x^2\text{.}\) Close, but the \(3\) went missing.
    🔗
    Easy enough. But now differentiate each of these:
    \begin{equation*} x^3, \qquad x^3 + 4, \qquad x^3 - 10, \qquad x^3 + 100. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.2. What do you notice?

    Find all four derivatives. What do you notice? So does knowing \(F'(x) = 3x^2\) pin down exactly one function?
    🔗
    Solution.
    All four have the same derivative: \(3x^2\text{.}\) The constant disappears when you differentiate. So no. Knowing \(F'(x) = 3x^2\) does not tell us which one we started with.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.2 What did the derivative forget?

    Let’s look at the whole family \(F(x) = x^3 + C\) at once. Drag the \(C\) slider. Then drag the \(x\) slider and watch the slope.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.4. Discuss with a neighbor.

    1. What changes when you move \(C\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What stays the same about the shape?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. At a fixed \(x\text{,}\) how do the slopes of the different curves compare?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Then finish the sentence: the derivative remembers the ________ but forgets the ________.
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. The curve slides up and down.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The shape does not change at all. Every curve is the same curve, shifted.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. They are identical. The slope depends on \(x\text{,}\) not on \(C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The derivative remembers the shape (the slope at every point) but forgets the vertical position.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.3 Now give the idea a name

    Definition 2.5.5. Antiderivative.

    A function \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) on an interval if \(F'(x) = f(x)\) for every \(x\) in that interval.
    🔗
    🔗
    So \(x^3\) is an antiderivative of \(3x^2\text{.}\) So is \(x^3 + 4\text{.}\) So is \(x^3 - 10\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To describe all of them at once, we write the most general antiderivative: \(F(x) = x^3 + C\text{,}\) where \(C\) can be any constant.
    🔗

    Fact 2.5.6. The family principle.

    If \(F\) is one antiderivative of \(f\) on an interval, then every antiderivative of \(f\) on that interval has the form \(F(x) + C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Why must we include the \(C\text{?}\) Because leaving it off throws away most of the family. You would be naming one curve, when the derivative only told you the shape.
    🔗

    Remark 2.5.7. A note on notation.

    You may have seen antiderivatives written with a special symbol somewhere. We are not using it yet, on purpose. That symbol has a deeper meaning, and we want you to see where it comes from first. It shows up in Chapter 3. For now, we write \(F\) with \(F' = f\text{,}\) and that is all we need.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.4 Running the derivative rules backward

    Start with the Power Rule. Differentiating \(x^{n+1}\) does two things: it multiplies by the exponent, then lowers the exponent by one.
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\left[x^{n+1}\right] = (n+1)x^n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To go backward, undo both steps in reverse order. Raise the exponent by one. Then divide by the new exponent.
    \begin{equation*} x^n \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C, \qquad n \neq -1. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.8. Why does this work?

    Differentiate \(\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C\text{.}\) Do you get back \(x^n\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C\right] = \frac{(n+1)x^n}{n+1} + 0 = x^n. \end{equation*}
    Yes. The \(n+1\) cancels, and the constant disappears.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    The one exception.

    What happens when \(n = -1\text{?}\) The formula would divide by \(n + 1 = 0\text{.}\) That breaks.
    🔗
    So for \(x^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{x}\text{,}\) we lean on a derivative we already know: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[\ln|x|\big] = \dfrac{1}{x}\text{.}\) So an antiderivative of \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) is \(\ln|x| + C\text{,}\) on any interval that does not contain \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.9. Build the toolkit yourself.

    Every derivative you know is secretly an antiderivative too. Find the most general antiderivative of each function. Then open the solution.
    🔗
    \(x^5\text{,}\)   \(x^{-3}\text{,}\)   \(x\text{,}\)   \(\dfrac{1}{x}\text{,}\)   \(e^x\text{,}\)   \(\sin x\text{,}\)   \(\cos x\text{,}\)   \(\sec^2 x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(f(x)\) Most general antiderivative
    \(x^5\) \(\dfrac{x^6}{6} + C\)
    \(x^{-3}\) \(\dfrac{x^{-2}}{-2} + C = -\dfrac{1}{2x^2} + C\)
    \(x\) \(\dfrac{x^2}{2} + C\)
    \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) \(\ln|x| + C\)
    \(e^x\) \(e^x + C\)
    \(\sin x\) \(-\cos x + C\)
    \(\cos x\) \(\sin x + C\)
    \(\sec^2 x\) \(\tan x + C\)
    The one people miss is \(\sin x\text{.}\) The derivative of \(\cos x\) is \(-\sin x\text{,}\) so we need the minus sign: \(-\cos x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Two more rules come along for free, because they work the same way for derivatives: constants stay put, and sums split into pieces.
    🔗

    Fact 2.5.10. The antiderivative toolkit.

    If \(F' = f\) and \(G' = g\text{,}\) and \(c\) is a constant, then:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} c\,f(x) &\;\longrightarrow\; c\,F(x) + C \\ f(x) \pm g(x) &\;\longrightarrow\; F(x) \pm G(x) + C \\ x^n &\;\longrightarrow\; \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \quad (n \neq -1) \\ \frac{1}{x} &\;\longrightarrow\; \ln|x| + C \\ e^x &\;\longrightarrow\; e^x + C \\ \sin x &\;\longrightarrow\; -\cos x + C \\ \cos x &\;\longrightarrow\; \sin x + C \\ \sec^2 x &\;\longrightarrow\; \tan x + C \end{align*}
    🔗

    Best error check today.

    Differentiate your answer. If you get back the function you started with, you are right.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.5 Finding the whole family

    Example 2.5.11. We do: one term at a time.

    Find the most general antiderivative of \(f(x) = 6x^2 - 4x + 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Work backward one term at a time. Raise the exponent, divide by the new exponent.
    \begin{align*} 6x^2 &\;\longrightarrow\; 6 \cdot \frac{x^3}{3} = 2x^3 \\ -4x &\;\longrightarrow\; -4 \cdot \frac{x^2}{2} = -2x^2 \\ 5 &\;\longrightarrow\; 5x \end{align*}
    🔗
    One \(+C\) covers the whole thing: \(F(x) = 2x^3 - 2x^2 + 5x + C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Check. \(F'(x) = 6x^2 - 4x + 5\text{.}\) It matches.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.12. Try one.

    Find the most general antiderivative of \(f(x) = 4x - 3x^2\text{.}\) Check your answer by differentiating before you open the solution.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(F(x) = 2x^2 - x^3 + C\text{.}\) Check: \(F'(x) = 4x - 3x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.6 A family is not always enough

    Suppose \(F'(x) = 3x^2\text{.}\) Then \(F(x) = x^3 + C\text{.}\) A whole family.
    🔗
    Now suppose we are also told \(F(1) = 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On a graph, that says: pick the one member of the family that passes through \((1, 5)\text{.}\) Go back to the family graph above. Set \(x = 1\text{,}\) then hunt with the \(C\) slider.
    🔗
    In algebra, plug in \(x = 1\text{:}\) \(\;5 = 1^3 + C\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.13. Pick the family member.

    🔗

    Remember.

    Derivative information \(+\) one condition \(\longrightarrow\) one particular function.
    🔗
    🔗
    A problem like this, with a derivative plus a starting value, is called an initial value problem.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.14. You do: select the right member of the family.

    🔗

    What if we are given the second derivative?

    Then we have to run backward twice. And every trip backward brings its own constant.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.15. Two steps back.

    🔗
    Why two constants? Each time we undo a derivative, we lose track of one vertical shift. Two derivatives, two lost shifts, two conditions to pin them down.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.7 Can we recover a function without a formula?

    This is an important shift. Sometimes there is no formula, only a graph.
    🔗
    Below is the graph of \(y = f(x)\text{,}\) and \(F' = f\text{.}\) So the graph of \(f\) tells you the slope of \(F\text{.}\) Also, \(F(0) = 2\text{.}\) Leave the checkbox alone for now.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.17. Read the slopes.

    1. Where is \(F\) increasing?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Where is \(F\) decreasing?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Where does \(F\) have horizontal tangents?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Where should \(F\) have a local minimum? A local maximum?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Now sketch one possible \(F\) on paper, starting at the point \((0, 2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(F\) increases where \(f \gt 0\text{:}\) between \(x = 1\) and \(x = 3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(F\) decreases where \(f \lt 0\text{:}\) from \(0\) to \(1\text{,}\) and from \(3\) to \(5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Where \(f = 0\text{:}\) at \(x = 1\) and \(x = 3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. A local minimum at \(x = 1\) (decreasing, then increasing) and a local maximum at \(x = 3\) (increasing, then decreasing).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Reading a graph of the slope.

    If \(F' = f\text{,}\) then \(f \gt 0\) means \(F\) increases, and \(f \lt 0\) means \(F\) decreases.
    🔗
    Zeros of \(f\) are where \(F\) has horizontal tangents.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.8 Our running example: a car

    Now back to a physical situation. A car has velocity \(v(t) = 3t^2\) meters per second. It starts at position \(s(0) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Velocity is the derivative of position: \(s'(t) = v(t) = 3t^2\text{.}\) So position is an antiderivative of velocity.
    🔗
    Recover the family. \(s(t) = t^3 + C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Use the starting position. \(s(0) = 0^3 + C = 0\text{,}\) so \(C = 0\) and \(s(t) = t^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So where is the car at \(t = 2\) seconds?
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.18. Where is the car?

    🔗

    Remember this number: 8.

    The car traveled \(s(2) - s(0) = 8\) meters.
    🔗
    We will meet this same car again.
    🔗
    🔗

    From acceleration all the way to position.

    Velocity is the derivative of position. Acceleration is the derivative of velocity. So we can run backward twice:
    \begin{equation*} a(t) \;\xrightarrow{\text{antidifferentiate}}\; v(t) \;\xrightarrow{\text{antidifferentiate}}\; s(t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.19. You do: a particle.

    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.9 Putting it together

    Checkpoint 2.5.20. Less scaffolding.

    1. Find the most general antiderivative of \(f(x) = 5x^4 - 3x^2 + 7\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find \(F\) if \(F'(x) = 4x^3 + 2x\) and \(F(1) = 3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. A particle has velocity \(v(t) = 6t - 4\) and \(s(0) = 10\text{.}\) Find its position function.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(F(x) = x^5 - x^3 + 7x + C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The family is \(x^4 + x^2 + C\text{.}\) Then \(F(1) = 2 + C = 3\text{,}\) so \(F(x) = x^4 + x^2 + 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(s(t) = 3t^2 - 4t + 10\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.21. Error analysis: who is most general?

    A class is asked for the most general antiderivative of \(F'(x) = 4x^3\text{.}\) Three students answer. Who gives the most general answer?
    🔗
    • Student A: \(F(x) = x^4\)
    • Student A found an antiderivative, and it is correct. But it is only one member of the family. The \(+C\) is missing.
    • Student B: \(F(x) = x^4 + C\)
    • Right. Correct function, and the \(+C\) captures the whole family.
    • Student C: \(F(x) = 4x^4 + C\)
    • Differentiate it: you get \(16x^3\text{.}\) Student C raised the exponent but forgot to divide by the new exponent.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.22. Exit ticket.

    1. In your own words, what is an antiderivative? Explain it using a car’s velocity and position.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Why does the general answer contain \(+C\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If \(F' = f\) and \(f \gt 0\) on an interval, is \(F\) increasing or decreasing there?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Why does an initial condition matter?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. An antiderivative of \(f\) is a function whose derivative is \(f\text{.}\) If \(f\) is a car’s velocity, an antiderivative is its position: you recover where the car is from how fast it is going.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Differentiating erases constants. So running backward can only recover the function up to a vertical shift.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Increasing. The slope of \(F\) is positive.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. It picks out the one member of the family we actually want, like knowing where the car started.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Main idea.

    Knowing a rate of change lets us work backward, but only to a family of functions.
    🔗
    The derivative remembers how the function changes. It forgets the vertical position. An initial condition tells us which member of the family we need.
