    Section 5.2 Volumes (Disks and Washers)

    Today’s Question.

    We found area by adding up many thin rectangles. Can we find a volume by adding up many thin pieces?
    🔗
    🔗
    Spoiler: yes. And the integral we get has exactly the same shape as the one for area. Only one ingredient changes.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.1 Start with something we already know

    Take a region under \(y = f(x)\) from \(x = a\) to \(x = b\text{.}\) Cut it into thin rectangles. A typical rectangle has height \(f(x)\) and width \(\Delta x\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \Delta A \approx f(x)\,\Delta x. \end{equation*}
    Add them up and let the rectangles get thinner:
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2.1. What are we actually adding?

    In \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\text{,}\) what are the tiny things being added together?
    🔗
    • Heights \(f(x)\)
    • Not quite. A height alone has no area. Something has to multiply it.
    • Areas of thin rectangles, \(f(x)\,\Delta x\)
    • Yes. Height times width. Keep that picture in your head.
    • Widths \(\Delta x\)
    • Adding only the widths gives \(b - a\text{.}\) That is the length of the interval, not the area.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.2 What is a slab?

    1. A familiar prism.

    How do you find the volume of a box, or any prism with straight sides? You need two things: the area of the face, \(A\text{,}\) and the thickness, \(h\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} V = (\text{area of face})(\text{thickness}) = Ah. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Make it very thin.

    Now picture one very thin piece of a more complicated solid. Its face at position \(x\) has area \(A(x)\text{.}\) Its thickness is \(\Delta x\text{.}\) So
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V \approx A(x)\,\Delta x. \end{equation*}
    Why only approximately? The solid changes a little from one side of the slab to the other, so the slab is not a perfect prism.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2.2. Predict.

    If the slab gets thinner, does \(A(x)\,\Delta x\) become a better or a worse estimate of the slab’s volume?
    🔗
    • Better
    • Right. A very thin slab barely changes from one side to the other, so it is almost exactly a prism.
    • Worse
    • Think about the Riemann rectangles in Section 4.1. Thinner rectangles gave better estimates. Same thing here.
    🔗

    3. Compare 2D and 3D.

    Put the two ideas side by side.
    🔗
    🔗
    Area Volume
    \(\Delta A \approx f(x)\,\Delta x\) \(\Delta V \approx A(x)\,\Delta x\)
    height \(f(x)\) cross-sectional area \(A(x)\)
    width \(\Delta x\) thickness \(\Delta x\)
    What changed? The height \(f(x)\) was replaced by the area of a cross-section, \(A(x)\text{.}\) What stayed the same? The \(\Delta x\text{.}\)
    🔗

    4. Many slabs, then thinner slabs.

    Cut the solid from \(x = a\) to \(x = b\) into \(n\) slabs and add:
    \begin{equation*} V \approx A(x_1^*)\,\Delta x + A(x_2^*)\,\Delta x + \cdots + A(x_n^*)\,\Delta x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} A(x_i^*)\,\Delta x. \end{equation*}
    That is a Riemann sum. The structure is identical to the one for area. As \(\Delta x \to 0\text{,}\) it becomes a definite integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    The big idea of this section.

    \begin{equation*} V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The hard part of a volume problem is usually not the integration. It is figuring out \(A(x)\text{,}\) the area of a typical cross-section.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.3 What happens when a rectangle spins?

    Here is where a lot of the solids in this section come from. Take a flat region, and spin it around an axis. It sweeps out a solid.
    🔗
    That sounds hard to picture. So do not try to picture the whole solid yet. Watch one thin rectangle.
    🔗
    Open animation in a new tab
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2.4. What do you notice?

    In the “Disk” example, the rectangle stands on the \(x\)-axis and reaches up to \(y = x^2\text{.}\) When it spins about the \(x\)-axis, what does it become, and what is its radius?
    🔗
    Solution.
    A thin disk. Its radius is the height of the rectangle, \(r(x) = x^2\text{,}\) because that is how far its top edge is from the axis.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Now give the idea a name. If a rectangle from the axis up to \(y = f(x)\) spins about the \(x\)-axis, it makes a disk with radius \(r(x) = f(x)\text{.}\) The face of that disk is a circle, so
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = \pi[r(x)]^2 = \pi[f(x)]^2 \end{equation*}
    and the big idea gives
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_a^b \pi[f(x)]^2\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Where did the square come from?

    Not from “it is a volume problem.” It comes from the geometry: the area of a circle is \(\pi r^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Warning 5.2.5.

    \(V = \pi\int r^2\,dx\) only works because the cross-section happens to be a circle. The general rule is still \(V = \int A(x)\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.4 We do: build the integral from the picture

    Rotate the region bounded by \(y = x^2\text{,}\) \(y = 0\text{,}\) \(x = 0\text{,}\) and \(x = 2\) about the \(x\)-axis. (This is the “Disk” example in Figure 5.2.3.)
    🔗
    1. Radius. The slice reaches from the axis to the curve: \(r(x) = x^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Area. \(A(x) = \pi(x^2)^2 = \pi x^4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Limits. \(0 \le x \le 2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Integral. \(V = \int_0^2 \pi x^4\,dx\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2.6. Try it.

    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.5 What if there is a hole?

    Now take a region trapped between two curves, \(y = R(x)\) on the outside and \(y = r(x)\) on the inside, with \(R(x) \ge r(x) \ge 0\text{.}\) Spin it about the \(x\)-axis.
    🔗
    Switch the animation to the “Washer” example and run step 3. The rectangle no longer touches the axis. There is a gap under it, and that gap spins into a hole. The cross-section is a washer: a big disk with a smaller disk removed.
    🔗
    So
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = \text{outer disk} - \text{inner disk} = \pi R^2 - \pi r^2 = \pi\left(R^2 - r^2\right) \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} V = \pi\int_a^b \left([R(x)]^2 - [r(x)]^2\right)dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Remember.

    Subtract areas, not radii. \(\pi(R - r)^2\) is not a washer.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2.7. Quick check.

    A washer has \(R = 3\) and \(r = 2\text{.}\) What is its area?
    🔗
    • \(\pi\)
    • That is \(\pi(3 - 2)^2\text{,}\) the area of a disk of radius 1. A washer is not a small disk.
    • \(5\pi\)
    • Yes. \(9\pi - 4\pi = 5\pi\text{.}\)
    • \(9\pi\)
    • That is the whole outer disk. You still need to remove the hole.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.6 We do: which radius is which?

    Rotate the region between \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\text{,}\) \(0 \le x \le 1\text{,}\) about the \(x\)-axis.
    🔗
    At a typical \(x\text{,}\) the line \(y = x\) is farther from the axis, so it gives the outer radius. The curve \(y = x^2\) is closer, so it gives the inner radius:
    \begin{equation*} R(x) = x, \qquad r(x) = x^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Notice the phrase. Not “\(x\) is bigger than \(x^2\text{.}\)” The reason is distance from the axis. That matters in a minute.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = \pi\left[x^2 - (x^2)^2\right] = \pi\left(x^2 - x^4\right), \qquad V = \pi\int_0^1 \left(x^2 - x^4\right)dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2.8. Try it.

    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.7 Distance to the axis matters

    Same region. Now spin it about the line \(y = 2\) instead. (The “Axis y = 2” example in the animation.)
    🔗
    Which curve is farther from the axis now? The axis sits above the region. So the lower curve \(y = x^2\) is the farther one.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2.9. Find the radii.

    Write the outer and inner radius as distances from \(y = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    A radius is a distance, so it is (axis) minus (curve) here:
    \begin{equation*} R(x) = 2 - x^2, \qquad r(x) = 2 - x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    So
    \begin{equation*} V = \pi\int_0^1 \left[(2 - x^2)^2 - (2 - x)^2\right]dx = \frac{8\pi}{15}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Remember.

    “Radius = the function” is a shortcut that only works when the axis is \(y = 0\text{.}\) The real rule: radius = distance from the axis of rotation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.8 Horizontal slices: the same idea

    Nothing about \(V = \int A\) requires \(dx\text{.}\) If the axis is vertical, the slices are horizontal, and everything becomes a function of \(y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_c^d A(y)\,dy. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Take the region between \(x = y^2\) and \(x = 4\text{,}\) \(-2 \le y \le 2\text{,}\) and spin it about the \(y\)-axis. (The “About y-axis” example.) A horizontal slice runs from \(x = y^2\) out to \(x = 4\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} R(y) = 4, \qquad r(y) = y^2, \qquad V = \pi\int_{-2}^{2}\left(16 - y^4\right)dy. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Which way do I slice?

    For disks and washers, slices are perpendicular to the axis. Horizontal axis: vertical slices, \(dx\text{.}\) Vertical axis: horizontal slices, \(dy\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.9 You do: set up before you integrate

    Checkpoint 5.2.10. A disk.

    Rotate the region bounded by \(y = \sqrt{x}\text{,}\) \(y = 0\text{,}\) and \(x = 4\) about the \(x\)-axis. Find \(r(x)\text{,}\) \(A(x)\text{,}\) and the integral for \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(r(x) = \sqrt{x}\text{,}\) so \(A(x) = \pi(\sqrt{x})^2 = \pi x\text{,}\) and \(V = \pi\int_0^4 x\,dx = 8\pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2.11. A washer.

    Rotate the region between \(y = 4\) and \(y = x^2\text{,}\) \(-2 \le x \le 2\text{,}\) about the \(x\)-axis. Find \(R(x)\text{,}\) \(r(x)\text{,}\) \(A(x)\text{,}\) and the integral for \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(R(x) = 4\) and \(r(x) = x^2\text{,}\) so \(A(x) = \pi(4)^2 - \pi(x^2)^2 = \pi(16 - x^4)\) and \(V = \pi\int_{-2}^{2}(16 - x^4)\,dx\text{.}\) By symmetry, \(2\pi\int_0^2 (16 - x^4)\,dx\) is also correct.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2.12. Less scaffolding.

    Rotate the region bounded by \(y = x^2\) and \(y = 4\) about the line \(y = 5\text{.}\) Set up the integral. Do not evaluate.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The curves meet at \(x = \pm 2\text{.}\) The axis is above the region, so the lower curve \(y = x^2\) is farther: \(R(x) = 5 - x^2\text{.}\) The line \(y = 4\) is closer: \(r(x) = 5 - 4 = 1\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} V = \pi\int_{-2}^{2}\left[(5 - x^2)^2 - 1\right]dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.10 Find the mistake

    Checkpoint 5.2.13. Error analysis.

    Three students rotate the region between \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\text{,}\) \(0 \le x \le 1\text{,}\) about the \(x\)-axis. Which setup is correct?
    🔗
    • Student A: \(V = \pi\int_0^1 (x - x^2)^2\,dx\)
    • Student A used \(x - x^2\) as a radius. That is the vertical thickness of the flat region, not a distance from the axis. The cross-section is a washer, not a disk.
    • Student B: \(V = \pi\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^4)\,dx\)
    • Yes. Outer disk minus inner disk: \(\pi x^2 - \pi x^4\text{.}\)
    • Student C: \(V = \int_0^1 \pi x^2 - \pi x^4\,dx\)
    • The idea is right, but the notation is not. Without parentheses, the \(dx\) only belongs to the last term. Write \(\int_0^1 \left(\pi x^2 - \pi x^4\right)dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.11 A setup checklist

    Before you integrate, ask:
    1. What is the axis of rotation?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Should my slices be vertical or horizontal?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. What does one slice become when it spins?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. What is the outer radius? Is there an inner radius?
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Are my radii actual distances from the axis?
      🔗
      🔗
    6. What are my limits, and does my differential match the slices?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Main idea.

    Draw, slice, radius, area, integral.
    🔗
    The main formula is not the disk formula or the washer formula. It is
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Disks and washers are just two ways of finding \(A(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
