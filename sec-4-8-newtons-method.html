    Section 3.2 Newton’s Method

    Today’s Question.

    Some equations have no formula for their solution. How do we solve them anyway?
    🔗
    🔗
    You can solve \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\text{.}\) Factor it, or use the quadratic formula.
    🔗
    Now try \(x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0\text{.}\) Or \(\cos x = x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For the first one, a formula does exist, but it is so messy that almost nobody uses it. For the second one, there is no formula at all. Yet both equations have a solution, and a calculator can find it in a blink. Let’s see how.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2.1 Curves are hard, lines are easy

    Here is the one idea behind this whole section.
    🔗
    Finding where a curve crosses the \(x\)-axis is hard. Finding where a line crosses it is easy.
    🔗
    And near any point, a smooth curve looks almost exactly like its tangent line. Zoom in far enough, and the curve straightens out.
    🔗
    So here is the plan. Replace the curve with its tangent line. Find where the line crosses the axis. Use that as a better guess. Repeat.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2.1. Warm-up: where does a line cross?

    🔗
    Hold onto that \(2.1\text{.}\) It is about to matter.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2.2 One step at a time

    Let \(f(x) = x^3 - 2x - 5\text{.}\) We want to solve \(f(x) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Make a first guess. \(f(2) = -1\) and \(f(3) = 16\text{.}\) The output changes sign between \(2\) and \(3\text{,}\) so a solution lives in there. And \(-1\) is much closer to \(0\) than \(16\) is. So start with \(x_1 = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Draw the tangent line at \(x_1\text{.}\) A line needs a point and a slope. The point is \((2, f(2)) = (2, -1)\text{.}\) The slope is \(f'(2)\text{.}\) Since \(f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2\text{,}\) the slope is \(f'(2) = 10\text{.}\)
    🔗
    That is exactly the line from the warm-up! It crosses the axis at \(x_2 = 2.1\text{.}\) That is our new guess.
    🔗
    Is it better? \(f(2.1) = 0.061\text{.}\) Much closer to \(0\) than \(-1\) was.
    🔗
    So do it again, starting from \(2.1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2.2. You do: take the next step.

    🔗
    Look how fast that went: \(x_1 = 2\text{,}\) \(x_2 = 2.1\text{,}\) \(x_3 \approx 2.0946\text{.}\) The true solution is \(2.09455\ldots\) Two steps, and the first four decimal places already match.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2.3 Now give the idea a name

    Let’s do one step with letters instead of numbers.
    🔗
    The tangent line at \(x_n\) goes through the point \((x_n, f(x_n))\) with slope \(f'(x_n)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} y = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x - x_n). \end{equation*}
    (That is the tangent line approximation, or linearization, from Calculus I.)
    🔗
    We want where it crosses the axis. So set \(y = 0\) and solve for \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} 0 &= f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x - x_n) \\ x - x_n &= -\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} \\ x &= x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}. \end{align*}
    That \(x\) is our next guess, \(x_{n+1}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Fact 3.2.4. Newton’s Method.

    To solve \(f(x) = 0\text{,}\) start with a guess \(x_1\text{.}\) Then repeat
    \begin{equation*} x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} \end{equation*}
    until the guesses stop changing, to the accuracy you need.
    🔗
    🔗

    Say it out loud.

    New guess equals old guess, minus height over slope.
    🔗
    The height \(f(x_n)\) says how far you are from zero. The slope \(f'(x_n)\) says how fast you are heading there.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2.5. Less scaffolding: where does cos x = x?

    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2.4 What could go wrong?

    Newton’s method is fast when it works. But it can fail. Think about the picture before you open the solution.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2.6. A flat tangent line.

    Suppose your guess lands where \(f'(x_n) = 0\text{.}\) Sketch the tangent line there. What happens to the next guess?
    🔗
    Solution.
    A flat tangent line never crosses the \(x\)-axis (unless you were already on it). In the formula, you would divide by \(0\text{.}\) There is no next guess.
    🔗
    Even a nearly flat tangent is trouble. It crosses the axis very far away, and the next guess can land nowhere near the solution. That is what you saw when you started the interactive near \(0.8\text{,}\) where \(f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2\) is close to \(0\text{.}\) The fix: pick a better first guess.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remember.

    A good first guess matters. Check where \(f\) changes sign, and start close.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2.7. Exit ticket.

    1. In your own words, what does one step of Newton’s method do?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Why do we use the tangent line instead of the curve?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Give one way Newton’s method can fail.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. It replaces the curve with its tangent line at the current guess, and uses the spot where that line crosses the axis as the next guess.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We cannot solve for where the curve crosses, but we can always solve for where a line crosses. And near the guess, the line is a good stand-in for the curve.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If \(f'(x_n) = 0\text{,}\) the tangent line is flat and never crosses the axis. A bad first guess can also send the guesses far away.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Main idea.

    When we cannot solve an equation, we solve an easier one instead: the tangent line. Repeat, and the answers close in on the real solution, fast.
