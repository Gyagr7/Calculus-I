    Section 5.1 Areas Between Curves

    Today’s Question.

    What does one thin piece measure?
    🔗
    🔗
    An integral is not just something to compute. It is a description of a picture: what we are adding up, where we start and stop, and which variable is changing. Today we read integrals as pictures, and then we go backward, from a picture to an integral.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.1 A familiar integral: can we see its meaning?

    Consider
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2 (3 - x)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Read it as the area under \(y = 3 - x\text{,}\) above the \(x\)-axis, from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 2\text{.}\) Do not evaluate yet.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.2. What do the bounds tell us?

    In \(\int_0^2 (3 - x)\,dx\text{,}\) what are the numbers \(0\) and \(2\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • Where the strips start and stop along the \(x\)-axis
    • Yes. The bounds say where the accumulation begins and ends.
    • The smallest and largest heights of the region
    • Not quite. The heights run from \(1\) to \(3\text{.}\) The bounds are \(x\)-values, not heights.
    🔗
    A typical strip has height \((3 - x) - 0 = 3 - x\) and width \(\Delta x\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.3. Predict.

    Is the area bigger than \(2\) and smaller than \(6\text{?}\) Explain using the picture, not a calculation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Yes. The width is \(2\) and every height is between \(1\) and \(3\text{.}\) So the region contains the \(2\)-by-\(1\) rectangle and fits inside the \(2\)-by-\(3\) rectangle: \(2 \lt A \lt 6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.4. Now evaluate.

    🔗

    What just happened.

    An integral tells us what we accumulate, where we begin and end, and which variable changes.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.2 What changes when the bottom boundary moves?

    Now consider
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2 \bigl[(3 - x) - 1\bigr]\,dx. \end{equation*}
    The region now sits between \(y = 3 - x\) and \(y = 1\text{.}\) Check the box in Figure 5.1.1 to raise the floor.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.5. Read the strip.

    🔗
    So \((3 - x) - 1\) measures the vertical distance from the lower boundary to the upper one. \(3 - x\) alone is only the top coordinate. The part from \(y = 0\) to \(y = 1\) is no longer in the region.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.6. Predict, then check.

    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.3 A coordinate is not a length

    A vertical segment runs from \(y = -3\) to \(y = -1\text{.}\) Its length is \((-1) - (-3) = 2\text{.}\) Both ends have negative coordinates. The length is still positive.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.7. True or false?

      A region that sits below the \(x\)-axis has negative area.
      🔗

    • True.

    • False. Geometric area is never negative. Negative coordinates tell us where the boundaries are. The difference, top minus bottom, tells us how long the strip is.

    • False.

    • False. Geometric area is never negative. Negative coordinates tell us where the boundaries are. The difference, top minus bottom, tells us how long the strip is.
    🔗
    One more thing. On \([0, 2]\text{,}\) the region between \(y = 3 - x\) and \(y = 0\text{,}\) and the region between \(y = 4 - x\) and \(y = 1\text{,}\) have the same strip height: \((3 - x) - 0 = (4 - x) - 1 = 3 - x\text{.}\) The second region is the first one shifted up. Same strips, same area.
    🔗

    Remember.

    The height of a vertical strip is the distance between its boundaries. Ask “Where is it?” and “How long is it?” as two separate questions.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.4 Can we go backward? From a picture to an integral

    Find the area of the region enclosed by
    \begin{equation*} y = x^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad y = 2x - x^2. \end{equation*}
    First describe the region. Then build the integral.
    🔗

    1. My prediction.

    The region fits inside a unit square, and it does not fill it. So \(0 \lt A \lt 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    2. My strip.

    At \(x = \frac12\text{,}\) the strip runs from \(y = \frac14\) up to \(y = \frac34\text{.}\) Its height is \(\frac12\text{.}\) In general:
    \begin{equation*} \text{height} = (2x - x^2) - x^2 = 2x - 2x^2, \qquad \text{width} = \Delta x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    3. My endpoints.

    The strip shrinks to nothing where the curves meet:
    \begin{equation*} x^2 = 2x - x^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x(x - 1) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 0, 1. \end{equation*}
    So the strips run from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 1\text{,}\) and in between, \(2x - x^2\) is on top.
    🔗
    🔗

    4. My small piece and my integral.

    One strip has area about \((2x - 2x^2)\,\Delta x\text{.}\) Add them up:
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_0^1 \bigl[(2x - x^2) - x^2\bigr]\,dx = \int_0^1 (2x - 2x^2)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.9. Evaluate.

    🔗

    Remember.

    Picture \(\to\) strip \(\to\) dimensions \(\to\) integral. The integral records how the small pieces build the whole region.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.5 Two shaded pieces: why might an integral give zero?

    Look at the region between \(y = x\) and \(y = x^3\) on \([-1, 1]\text{.}\) It comes in two pieces. Make a prediction before you integrate anything.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.11. Predict.

    Do the two pieces have the same area? Could the total area be zero?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Same area: rotating the picture \(180^\circ\) about the origin swaps the two pieces. But the total cannot be zero. Each piece is made of strips with positive length.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Now read two strips off the picture:
    🔗
    \(x\) top bottom strip height
    \(-\frac12\) \(y = -\frac18\) (on \(y = x^3\)) \(y = -\frac12\) (on \(y = x\)) \(\frac38\)
    \(\frac12\) \(y = \frac12\) (on \(y = x\)) \(y = \frac18\) (on \(y = x^3\)) \(\frac38\)
    A student proposes
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_{-1}^{1} (x - x^3)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.12. What does the student’s integral give?

    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.13. What cancelled?

    Why did the student’s integral come out to \(0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • The region really has no area
    • Look at the picture. Both pieces have strips with positive length.
    • On the left piece, \(x - x^3\) is negative, so it cancels the right piece
    • Yes. On \((-1, 0)\) the curves trade places: \(x^3\) is on top. So \(x - x^3\) is bottom minus top there, a negative number. At \(x = -\frac12\) it is \(-\frac38\text{,}\) while the strip is \(\frac38\) long.
    • The bounds should have been \(0\) and \(1\)
    • That would only count the right piece. The left piece is part of the region too.
    🔗

    Remember.

    A strip has a nonnegative height. Before you use one subtraction everywhere, check whether the boundaries trade roles.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.6 Let the picture tell us where to split

    Solve \(x = x^3\text{:}\) \(x(x - 1)(x + 1) = 0\text{,}\) so \(x = -1, 0, 1\text{.}\) At the inside intersection, \(x = 0\text{,}\) the top and bottom curves swap. So describe each piece separately:
    🔗
    interval top curve bottom curve height
    \([-1, 0]\) \(x^3\) \(x\) \(x^3 - x\)
    \([0, 1]\) \(x\) \(x^3\) \(x - x^3\)
    Then add the areas:
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_{-1}^{0} (x^3 - x)\,dx + \int_{0}^{1} (x - x^3)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.14. Use the symmetry, then evaluate.

    🔗

    Fact 5.1.15. Area between curves.

    If \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous and \(f(x) \ge g(x)\) on \([a, b]\text{,}\) the area between them is
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_a^b \underbrace{\bigl[f(x) - g(x)\bigr]}_{\text{strip height}}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    If the curves trade places, describe each piece separately and add the areas.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.7 Your turn: both boundaries below the axis

    Checkpoint 5.1.16.

    The region lies between \(y = -x^2 - 1\) and \(y = -3\) for \(-1 \le x \le 1\text{.}\) Sketch it and draw a strip. Should the area be positive or negative? Write an integral for the area and explain why your strip height is never negative. Do not evaluate.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The curve \(y = -x^2 - 1\) is on top. Height \(= (-x^2 - 1) - (-3) = 2 - x^2\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_{-1}^{1} (2 - x^2)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Since \(x^2 \le 1\) on \([-1, 1]\text{,}\) every strip is at least \(1\) long. The area is positive even though every coordinate is negative. Note the side boundaries are the given lines \(x = \pm 1\text{,}\) not where the curves cross.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.8 Does the direction of the strip matter?

    Consider the region enclosed by
    \begin{equation*} x = y^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x = y + 2. \end{equation*}
    Try strips in both directions.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.18. Vertical strips.

    Look at vertical strips at \(x = \frac12\) and at \(x = 2\text{.}\) Which curve gives the top of each strip? Which gives the bottom? Does the bottom stay on the same curve all the way across?
    🔗
    Solution.
    At \(x = \frac12\text{,}\) both ends are on the parabola: \(y = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\text{.}\) At \(x = 2\text{,}\) the bottom is on the line (\(y = 0\)) and the top is on the parabola (\(y = \sqrt2\)). The top is always \(y = \sqrt{x}\text{.}\) The bottom is \(y = -\sqrt{x}\) for \(0 \le x \le 1\text{,}\) then \(y = x - 2\) for \(1 \le x \le 4\text{.}\) It changes at \((1, -1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.19. Horizontal strips.

    🔗

    Remember.

    Both directions can work. Choose the one that makes the dimensions of the pieces easier to describe.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.9 Build an integral from horizontal pieces

    Stay with \(x = y^2\) and \(x = y + 2\text{,}\) and use horizontal strips.
    1. Dimensions. Length \(= x_{\text{right}} - x_{\text{left}} = (y + 2) - y^2\text{.}\) Thickness \(= \Delta y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Small piece. Area \(\approx \bigl[(y + 2) - y^2\bigr]\,\Delta y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Bounds. \(y^2 = y + 2\) gives \((y - 2)(y + 1) = 0\text{,}\) so \(y = -1\) and \(y = 2\text{.}\) The variable changing is \(y\text{,}\) so these are the bounds.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Integral. \(A = \int_{-1}^{2} \bigl[(y + 2) - y^2\bigr]\,dy\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.20. Evaluate.

    🔗
    Vertical strips work too. They just need two integrals, because the bottom curve changes at \(x = 1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_0^1 2\sqrt{x}\,dx + \int_1^4 \bigl[\sqrt{x} - (x - 2)\bigr]\,dx = \frac43 + \frac{19}{6} = \frac92. \end{equation*}
    Same region, same area. The two setups only reflect how the strip boundaries are described.
    🔗

    Same reasoning, a different direction.

    strip length thickness
    vertical \(y_{\text{top}} - y_{\text{bottom}}\) \(dx\)
    horizontal \(x_{\text{right}} - x_{\text{left}}\) \(dy\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.10 Exit ticket: make the geometry visible

    Checkpoint 5.1.21.

    The region is enclosed by \(x = 1 + y^2\) and \(x = 3 - y^2\text{.}\) Do not evaluate.
    1. Can the area be negative or zero? Explain without calculating.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Choose a strip direction and say why.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Write both dimensions of your strip.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Find the bounds.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Write an integral for the area. Do you need more than one?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The area is positive: the region has positive width and height. Horizontal strips are easiest, because the left curve is always \(x = 1 + y^2\) and the right curve is always \(x = 3 - y^2\text{.}\) Length \(= (3 - y^2) - (1 + y^2) = 2 - 2y^2\text{,}\) thickness \(\Delta y\text{.}\) The curves meet where \(y^2 = 1\text{,}\) at \((2, -1)\) and \((2, 1)\text{.}\) One integral is enough:
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_{-1}^{1} (2 - 2y^2)\,dy. \end{equation*}
    A vertical setup also works, but needs two integrals split at \(x = 2\text{:}\) \(\int_1^2 2\sqrt{x - 1}\,dx + \int_2^3 2\sqrt{3 - x}\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1.22. One final question.

    Why can an integral equal zero even though the area between two curves is positive?
    🔗
    Solution.
    An integral of one fixed difference can have positive and negative parts that cancel. Area adds up strip lengths, which are never negative, using the correct top and bottom on each part.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Main idea.

    Read the picture. Draw a piece. Describe its dimensions. Check whether that description works across the whole region. Then write the integral that adds the pieces.
