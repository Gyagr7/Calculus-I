    Section 2.1 Derivative Rules and Critical Points

    Subsection 2.1.1 What does a derivative actually mean?

    Today’s Question.

    Your speedometer says \(45\) mph. But at a single instant, no time passes. So what is that \(45\) measuring?
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is why that is strange. Speed is distance divided by time. At one instant, the time is zero. And you cannot divide by zero.
    🔗
    Yet the needle points at a real number. Where does it come from?
    🔗

    Start with something you can compute.

    You can compute an average speed. Pick a start time and an end time, then divide:
    \begin{equation*} \text{average speed} = \frac{\text{change in position}}{\text{change in time}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Say your position after \(t\) seconds is \(f(t) = t^2\) meters. We want your speed at exactly \(t = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Here is the first average, done for you. From \(t = 1\) to \(t = 2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{f(2) - f(1)}{2 - 1} = \frac{4 - 1}{1} = 3 \text{ m/s}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A one-second window is pretty wide, though. Let’s shrink it. Your turn.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.1. Shrink the window.

    🔗
    See a pattern yet? Push it one more step.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.2. One more squeeze.

    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.3. What do you notice?

    Line up your averages, from the widest window to the narrowest. What is happening to them? Decide before you open the solution.
    🔗
    Solution.
    They go \(3\text{,}\) \(2.1\text{,}\) \(2.01\text{,}\) \(2.001\text{.}\) Each one is closer to \(2\text{.}\) They are not jumping around. They are settling down.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    What just happened.

    As the window shrinks, the average speed settles on one number: \(2\) m/s. That number is your speed at the instant \(t = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Notice what we did not do. We never divided by zero. We just watched where the averages were headed.
    🔗
    🔗

    Now see it on a graph.

    The same squeeze has a picture. It is the picture to keep in your head all semester.
    🔗
    Pick a point \(P\) on a curve. Pick a second point \(Q\) nearby. Draw the line through them. That is a secant line. Its slope is an average rate of change, just like the averages you computed.
    🔗
    Now slide \(Q\) toward \(P\text{.}\) Before you touch the graph, predict: what will happen to the line? What will happen to its slope?
    🔗
    🔗
    The secant line swings around and settles onto one line: the line that just touches the curve at \(P\text{.}\) That is the tangent line.
    🔗
    And its slope? \(2\text{.}\) The same number you found with the table. That is not a coincidence. The table and the picture are the same squeeze.
    🔗

    Now give the idea a name.

    Twice now, we did the same thing: shrink a window, and watch an average rate of change settle down. That settling number has a name.
    🔗
    🔗

    Insight 2.1.5. The big idea.

    A derivative is the number an average rate of change settles on as the window shrinks to a single point.
    🔗
    Everything else in this chapter is bookkeeping on top of that one idea.
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is the same idea, written in symbols.
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.6. The derivative.

    The derivative of \(f\) at \(x\text{,}\) written \(f'(x)\text{,}\) is
    \begin{equation*} f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}, \end{equation*}
    provided the limit exists.
    🔗
    🔗
    Check that it matches what you did. The fraction \(\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\) is an average rate of change over a window of width \(h\text{.}\) The \(\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0}\) part just says: shrink the window.
    🔗

    Two ways to read \(f'(x)\).

    On a graph, \(f'(x)\) is the slope of the tangent line. Use this reading when you care about steepness.
    🔗
    In the world, \(f'(x)\) is an instantaneous rate of change. Use this reading when you care about how fast something is happening right now.
    🔗
    Velocity is the derivative of position. If \(C(t)\) is the concentration of a drug in the bloodstream, then \(C'(t)\) tells you how fast that level is rising or falling right now. Same computation. Different story.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.7. True or false?

      \(f'(x)\) tells you the average rate of change of \(f\) over the interval from \(x\) to \(x+1\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • False. \(f'(x)\) is the rate at the single point \(x\text{.}\) Once the limit is taken, there is no window left to average over. The average over \([x,x+1]\) is a different number, \(\dfrac{f(x+1)-f(x)}{1}\text{,}\) and it usually does not match \(f'(x)\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • False. \(f'(x)\) is the rate at the single point \(x\text{.}\) Once the limit is taken, there is no window left to average over. The average over \([x,x+1]\) is a different number, \(\dfrac{f(x+1)-f(x)}{1}\text{,}\) and it usually does not match \(f'(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 2.1.8. We do: the definition, by hand.

    Let \(f(x) = x^2\text{.}\) Use Definition 2.1.6 to find \(f'(x)\text{.}\) Then check: does \(f'(1)\) match the \(2\) we found twice already?
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, a trap. If we plug in \(h = 0\) right away, we get \(\dfrac{0}{0}\text{.}\) That tells us nothing. So we simplify first and take the limit last.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h) \\ &= 2x. \end{align*}
    Once the \(h\) in the denominator cancels, letting \(h \to 0\) is safe.
    🔗
    So \(f'(x) = 2x\text{,}\) and \(f'(1) = 2\text{.}\) Same number, third time.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    That worked nicely for \(x^2\text{.}\) Now imagine doing it for \(x^{10}\text{.}\) Or for \(\sin x \cos x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    No thanks.
    🔗
    That is why we have rules. Each rule is this same limit, worked out once, for a whole family of functions.
    🔗

    Keep these two apart.

    The definition tells you what a derivative is.
    🔗
    The rules tell you how to compute one fast.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.2 The differentiation toolkit

    You already know these rules. Here is a better question: do you know why they work?
    🔗
    If you know why, you will not mix them up. So for each rule, we start with a guess or a picture. The formula comes second.
    🔗

    Three rules you could guess.

    Try these before you open the solution. No formulas needed.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.9. Predict first.

    1. The function \(f(x) = 7\) never changes. How fast is it changing?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. You triple every output of a function. What happens to how fast it changes?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. You have two savings accounts, each growing on its own. How fast does the total grow?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Not at all. Its rate of change is \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. It triples too. Three times the output means three times the change.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Add the two rates. The total grows at the combined rate.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    If you got those, you already know three rules. Here they are in symbols.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \text{Constant Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[c] = 0 \\ \text{Constant Multiple Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[c \, f(x)] = c \, f'(x) \\ \text{Sum/Difference Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[f(x) \pm g(x)] = f'(x) \pm g'(x) \end{align*}

    The Power Rule, as a picture.

    Think of \(x^3\) as three copies of \(x\) multiplied together: \(x \cdot x \cdot x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now nudge \(x\) up a tiny bit. Each of the three copies gets nudged. Each nudge adds about \(x^2\) worth of change (the other two copies, multiplied).
    🔗
    Three copies. Three nudges. Total rate of change: \(3x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    With \(n\) copies, you get \(n\) nudges, each worth about \(x^{n-1}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Power Rule:} \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[x^n] = n\,x^{n-1} \end{equation*}
    The picture works for whole numbers \(n\text{.}\) The rule works for any exponent: negative, fractional, anything. Let’s put that to the test.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.10. Error analysis: who is right?

    Four students differentiated \(x^{-2}\text{.}\) Which answer is correct?
    🔗
    • Student A: \(-2x^{-1}\)
    • Student A lowered the exponent in the wrong direction. Subtract \(1\text{:}\) \(-2-1=-3\text{,}\) not \(-1\text{.}\)
    • Student B: \(-2x^{-3}\)
    • Right. Bring the \(-2\) down in front. Then lower the exponent by one: \(-2\) becomes \(-3\text{.}\)
    • Student C: \(2x^{-3}\)
    • Student C got the exponent right but lost the minus sign. The number in front is the old exponent, \(-2\text{.}\)
    • Student D: \(-2x^{-1}\cdot(-2)\)
    • Student D mixed two rules together. The Power Rule brings the exponent down once and lowers it by \(1\text{.}\) Nothing gets multiplied twice.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.11. You do: two quick ones.

    🔗

    The Product Rule, as a picture.

    Picture a rectangle. Its width is \(u(x)\) and its height is \(v(x)\text{.}\) So its area is \(u(x)\,v(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now nudge \(x\text{.}\) Both sides grow a little. Where does the new area show up?
    🔗
    In two thin strips. One strip comes from the width growing: about \(u' \cdot v\text{.}\) The other comes from the height growing: about \(u \cdot v'\text{.}\) There is also a tiny corner piece, but it is so small that it disappears in the limit.
    🔗
    Add the two strips, and that is the whole rule:
    🔗
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Product Rule:} \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[u\,v] = u'\,v + u\,v' \end{equation*}
    In words: differentiate one factor at a time, hold the other one fixed, and add.
    🔗

    The Quotient Rule is not really new.

    It is the Product Rule in disguise. Write \(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) as \(f(x) \cdot g(x)^{-1}\text{.}\) Apply the Product Rule, plus the Chain Rule for \(g(x)^{-1}\text{,}\) and the familiar formula falls out. That is also why the minus sign and the \(g^2\) show up where they do.
    🔗
    🔗

    The Chain Rule: rates multiply.

    This is usually the rustiest rule. It is also the one we lean on hardest this semester. Substitution, our main integration technique in Chapter 4, is the Chain Rule run backward.
    🔗
    So let’s rebuild it from something simple: gears.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.12. Gears.

    🔗
    A composition \(f(g(x))\) works exactly like those gears.
    🔗
    Nudge \(x\text{,}\) and the inner function \(g(x)\) changes at rate \(g'(x)\text{.}\) That change makes the outer function change at rate \(f'\text{,}\) measured at \(g(x)\text{.}\) Two rates in a chain. So multiply:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Chain Rule:} \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big[f(g(x))\big] = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x) \end{equation*}

    Watch out.

    Two places people slip: forgetting the \(g'(x)\) at the end, and plugging \(x\) into \(f'\) instead of \(g(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    A few derivatives to know by heart.

    Two of these deserve a picture instead of memorizing.
    🔗
    \(e^x\) is its own rate of change. The bigger it gets, the faster it grows, like money under continuous compounding.
    🔗
    Differentiating a wave gives back a wave, shifted by a quarter-cycle. The slope of \(\sin x\) traces out \(\cos x\text{.}\) The slope of \(\cos x\) traces out \(-\sin x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Fact 2.1.13. Standard derivatives.

    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}[\sin x] &= \cos x & \frac{d}{dx}[\cos x] &= -\sin x \\ \frac{d}{dx}[\tan x] &= \sec^2 x & \frac{d}{dx}[e^x] &= e^x \\ \frac{d}{dx}[\ln x] &= \frac{1}{x} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Example 2.1.14. We do: Product Rule and Chain Rule together.

    Differentiate \(f(x) = x^2 e^{3x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    What kind of function is this? A product: \(x^2\) times \(e^{3x}\text{.}\) So the Product Rule goes first.
    🔗
    Does either piece need another rule? Yes. \(e^{3x}\) is a composition. The outer function is \(e^{(\;\cdot\;)}\text{.}\) The inner function is \(3x\text{,}\) with derivative \(3\text{.}\) So \(\frac{d}{dx}\big[e^{3x}\big] = 3e^{3x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now put it together.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f'(x) &= \frac{d}{dx}[x^2]\cdot e^{3x} + x^2 \cdot \frac{d}{dx}[e^{3x}] \\ &= 2x\,e^{3x} + x^2 \cdot 3e^{3x} \\ &= x e^{3x}(2 + 3x). \end{align*}
    Both terms had an \(x\,e^{3x}\) in them, so we factored it out.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.15. You do: Quotient Rule.

    Differentiate \(g(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x - 3}\text{.}\) Work it out on paper before you open the solution.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} g'(x) &= \frac{(2x)(x-3) - (x^2+1)(1)}{(x-3)^2} \\ &= \frac{2x^2 - 6x - x^2 - 1}{(x-3)^2} \\ &= \frac{x^2 - 6x - 1}{(x-3)^2}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is the whole toolkit on one card. Use it as a reference. When you forget a rule, go back to its picture.
    🔗

    Fact 2.1.16. The differentiation toolkit, all in one place.

    For differentiable functions \(f\) and \(g\text{,}\) and a constant \(c\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \text{Constant Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[c] = 0 \\ \text{Power Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[x^n] = n x^{n-1} \\ \text{Constant Multiple Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[c \, f(x)] = c \, f'(x) \\ \text{Sum/Difference Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[f(x) \pm g(x)] = f'(x) \pm g'(x) \\ \text{Product Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)g(x)] = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x) \\ \text{Quotient Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}\!\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2} \\ \text{Chain Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}\big[f(g(x))\big] = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x) \end{align*}
    🔗

    Main idea.

    Every rule is the definition of the derivative, worked out once for a whole family of functions. Remember the picture, and you can rebuild the rule.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.3 Critical points

    Question.

    You are hiking a trail shaped like the graph of \(f\text{.}\) You reach the very top of a hill. What is the ground like under your feet?
    🔗
    🔗
    Most of the time, the ground is tilted. You are climbing, or you are heading down. Walk the trail below. Watch the slope at the hilltop and at the bottom of the valley.
    🔗
    At the top of the hill, the ground is flat. For one instant, the slope is \(0\text{.}\) Then it tips the other way. The bottom of a valley works the same way.
    🔗
    In symbols, a flat spot at \(x = c\) means \(f'(c) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    One more case: sharp corners.

    Some peaks are not smooth. Picture the graph of \(y = -|x|\text{.}\) Its top, at \(x = 0\text{,}\) is a sharp point. What is the slope right there?
    🔗
    There isn’t one. Just left of \(0\text{,}\) the slope is \(1\text{.}\) Just right of \(0\text{,}\) it is \(-1\text{.}\) So \(f'(0)\) does not exist. But it is still a peak, so we want to count it too.
    🔗
    🔗
    Now give both cases one name.
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.18. Critical point.

    A critical point of \(f\) is a value \(c\) in the domain of \(f\) where either \(f'(c) = 0\) (flat ground) or \(f'(c)\) does not exist (a sharp corner or a break).
    🔗
    🔗
    (Your textbook calls these critical numbers. Same idea.)
    🔗
    So every hilltop and valley bottom along the trail (not at its very ends) is a critical point. Does it work the other way? Is every critical point a hilltop or a valley? Decide before you check.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.19. True or false?

      If \(f'(c) = 0\text{,}\) then \(f\) has a local maximum or a local minimum at \(c\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • False. Try \(f(x) = x^3\text{.}\) Its derivative is \(3x^2\text{,}\) so \(f'(0) = 0\) and the ground is flat at \(x = 0\text{.}\) But the graph keeps climbing on both sides. No peak, no valley. Think of a trail that levels off for one step, then keeps going up.

    • False.

    • False. Try \(f(x) = x^3\text{.}\) Its derivative is \(3x^2\text{,}\) so \(f'(0) = 0\) and the ground is flat at \(x = 0\text{.}\) But the graph keeps climbing on both sides. No peak, no valley. Think of a trail that levels off for one step, then keeps going up.
    🔗

    Remember.

    A critical point is a candidate, not automatically an answer.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.1.20. We do: find the critical points.

    Find the critical points of \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Is there anywhere \(f'\) fails to exist? No. \(f\) is a polynomial, so it has no corners or breaks. That leaves only the flat spots.
    🔗
    Where is the ground flat? Set \(f'(x) = 0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f'(x) &= 3x^2 - 3 \\ 0 &= 3x^2 - 3 \\ 0 &= 3(x^2-1) \\ x &= \pm 1. \end{align*}
    So the critical points are \(x=-1\) and \(x=1\text{.}\) Sketch the graph and you will see a hilltop at \(x=-1\) and a valley at \(x=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.21. You do: find the critical points.

    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.22. Exit ticket.

    Answer in your own words. One sentence each is plenty.
    1. What is a derivative? No formulas allowed.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If we have the definition, why do we need the rules?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Is every critical point a peak or a valley? Give an example.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. One good answer: how fast something is changing at a single instant, found by shrinking an average rate of change down to one point.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The definition is slow. Each rule is the definition worked out once, so we never have to redo the limit by hand.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. No. \(f(x) = x^3\) has a critical point at \(x = 0\text{,}\) but no peak or valley there.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Main idea.

    Peaks and valleys can only happen where the slope is \(0\) or does not exist. Those points are candidates. Deciding which candidate is the best is what optimization is about.
    🔗
    🔗
    That is exactly where we go next, in Chapter 3. There, “find the critical points” becomes one step in solving real problems, like building the biggest possible box from a sheet of cardboard.
