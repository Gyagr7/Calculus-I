    Section 1.1 Derivative Rules and Critical Points

    Subsection 1.1.1 What does a derivative actually mean?

    Picture yourself driving. The odometer racks up total distance, and dividing that by elapsed time gives your average speed for the whole trip — one honest number, easy to compute. The speedometer is answering a stranger question: how fast are you going right now, at this exact instant? There’s no stretch of time to divide by here — time isn’t passing at an instant. And yet the needle gives a real, meaningful number. Figuring out what that number even means, and how to compute it for any changing quantity — not just a car’s position, but a population, a temperature, an account balance — is the entire point of a derivative.
    🔗
    Here’s the trick that makes "speed right now" computable: you can’t measure it directly, but you can measure average speed over a short window of time, and see what happens as that window shrinks toward nothing. Say your position after \(t\) seconds is \(f(t) = t^2\) (in some units), and you want your speed exactly at \(t=1\text{.}\) Average speed over \([1, 1+h]\) is \(\dfrac{f(1+h)-f(1)}{h}\text{.}\) Watch what happens as \(h\) shrinks:
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} h = 1: \qquad \dfrac{f(2)-f(1)}{1} = \dfrac{4-1}{1} = 3 \\ h = 0.1: \qquad \dfrac{f(1.1)-f(1)}{0.1} = \dfrac{1.21-1}{0.1} = 2.1 \\ h = 0.01: \qquad \dfrac{f(1.01)-f(1)}{0.01} = \dfrac{1.0201-1}{0.01} = 2.01 \end{gather*}
    The average speeds aren’t jumping around — they’re homing in on \(2\text{.}\) That limiting number, the one the average settles toward as the time window shrinks to a single instant, is the instantaneous speed at \(t=1\text{.}\) Nothing mystical is happening; we’re just squeezing an average over a smaller and smaller window until, in the limit, it stops being an average of two points and becomes a property of one.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.1.1. Try it yourself.

    🔗
    The same squeeze has a geometric picture, and it’s the one worth keeping in your head all semester. Take any curve \(y=f(x)\) and a point on it. Pick a second, nearby point, and draw the line through both — a secant line. Its slope is exactly the average rate of change we computed above, \(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\text{.}\) Now let that second point slide back toward the first, so \(h \to 0\text{.}\) The secant line swings around and settles onto the tangent line: the line that just grazes the curve at that single point, tilted at exactly the curve’s own steepness there. That settling slope is the derivative.
    🔗

    Insight 1.1.3. The big idea.

    A derivative is nothing more than: the slope an average rate of change settles into as the interval it’s averaged over shrinks to a single point. Everything in this subsection — the formula below, the rules in the next subsection, all of it — is just bookkeeping built on top of that one idea.
    🔗
    🔗
    Writing that squeeze down symbolically, instead of re-deriving it with a table of numbers every time, gives the formal definition:
    🔗

    Definition 1.1.4. The derivative.

    The derivative of \(f\) at \(x\text{,}\) written \(f'(x)\text{,}\) is
    \begin{equation*} f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}, \end{equation*}
    provided the limit exists.
    🔗
    🔗
    You’ll hear \(f'(x)\) described two ways, and both readings trace straight back to the picture above. Geometrically, it’s the slope of the tangent line to \(y=f(x)\) at \(x\) — useful whenever you care about steepness. Physically, it’s an instantaneous rate of change — useful whenever you care about how fast something is happening right now. Velocity is the derivative of position; marginal cost is the derivative of total cost; the rate an epidemic spreads is the derivative of case count. Same computation, different story attached to it depending on what \(f\) represents.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.1.5. True or false?

      \(f'(x)\) tells you the average rate of change of \(f\) over the interval from \(x\) to \(x+1\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • False. \(f'(x)\) is the instantaneous rate of change at the single point \(x\) — there’s no interval left to average over once the limit has been taken. The average rate of change over \([x,x+1]\) is the different number \(\dfrac{f(x+1)-f(x)}{1}\text{,}\) and generally won’t match \(f'(x)\) at all.

    • False.

    • False. \(f'(x)\) is the instantaneous rate of change at the single point \(x\) — there’s no interval left to average over once the limit has been taken. The average rate of change over \([x,x+1]\) is the different number \(\dfrac{f(x+1)-f(x)}{1}\text{,}\) and generally won’t match \(f'(x)\) at all.
    🔗

    Example 1.1.6. Computing a derivative directly from the definition.

    Let \(f(x) = x^2\text{.}\) Find \(f'(x)\) using Definition 1.1.4, and check it against the numerical squeeze above.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Set up the difference quotient and simplify before taking the limit — plugging in \(h=0\) too early gives the meaningless \(0/0\text{,}\) since we need the algebra to cancel that \(h\) out of the denominator first.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h) \\ &= 2x. \end{align*}
    Now \(h\) can safely go to \(0\text{,}\) since it’s no longer sitting in a denominator. At \(x=1\) this gives \(f'(1) = 2\) — exactly the number our shrinking-window table was homing in on. The definition isn’t just abstract machinery; it’s the same squeeze, done symbolically instead of numerically, for every \(x\) at once.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    That algebra worked fine for \(x^2\text{,}\) but imagine repeating it for \(f(x) = x^{10}\text{,}\) or \(f(x) = \sin(x)\cos(x)\text{.}\) The definition is the truth of what a derivative is — you should always be able to fall back on it — but it’s a slow way to compute one. The rules below exist purely so you never have to run that limit by hand again; every single one of them is just this same limit, worked out once and in general, for a whole family of functions at a time.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.2 The differentiation toolkit

    Rather than list these as symbols to memorize, here’s the intuition behind each one — the picture that makes the formula feel inevitable rather than arbitrary. A clean reference table for all of them sits at the end of this subsection once the ideas are back in place.
    🔗
    Three of the rules are close to obvious once you see them stated. A constant function never changes, so its rate of change is \(0\text{.}\) Scaling a function by a constant factor scales its rate of change by that same factor — if you triple every output, you triple how fast it’s changing too. And if two quantities change independently and you add them together (two separate savings accounts, each earning its own interest), the combined total changes at the combined rate — you just add the two rates.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \text{Constant Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[c] = 0 \\ \text{Constant Multiple Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[c \, f(x)] = c \, f'(x) \\ \text{Sum/Difference Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[f(x) \pm g(x)] = f'(x) \pm g'(x) \end{align*}
    The Power Rule takes a little more picturing. Think of \(x^n\) as \(n\) copies of \(x\) multiplied together. Nudge \(x\) up by a tiny amount, and each of those \(n\) copies gets nudged — each contributing its own small sliver to the product. With \(n\) nudges each worth roughly \(x^{n-1}\) stacking up, the total rate of change comes out to \(n\) times \(x^{n-1}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Power Rule:} \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[x^n] = n\,x^{n-1} \end{equation*}
    (That picture is easiest to see for a whole number \(n\text{,}\) but the formula itself holds for any real exponent — negative, fractional, whatever you throw at it.)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.1.7. Quick check: Power Rule.

    Which of these is \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[x^{-2}\right]\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(-2x^{-1}\)
    • Careful with the exponent step: the Power Rule drops the exponent by \(1\text{,}\) so \(-2\) becomes \(-2-1=-3\text{,}\) not \(-1\text{.}\)
    • \(-2x^{-3}\)
    • Right — bring the \(-2\) down as a coefficient, then drop the exponent by one: \(-2\) becomes \(-3\text{.}\)
    • \(2x^{-3}\)
    • The exponent change is right, but the sign of the coefficient should carry over from the original \(-2\text{.}\)
    • \(-2x^{-1}\cdot(-2)\)
    • That mixes up two different rules. The Power Rule just brings the exponent down as a coefficient and lowers it by \(1\) — nothing gets multiplied twice.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.1.8. Two more Power Rule reflexes.

    🔗
    The Product Rule has a nice picture too. Imagine a rectangle whose width is \(u(x)\) and whose height is \(v(x)\text{,}\) so its area is \(u(x)v(x)\text{.}\) Nudge \(x\) slightly, and both sides grow a little. The extra area shows up as two thin strips — one along the top from the width growing (worth \(u' \cdot v\)) and one along the side from the height growing (worth \(u \cdot v'\)) — plus a tiny corner sliver so small it vanishes in the limit. Add the two strips and you have the whole rule: differentiate one factor at a time, holding the other one fixed, and add the results.
    🔗
    The Quotient Rule isn’t really a separate idea — it’s the Product Rule wearing a disguise. Write \(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) as \(f(x)\cdot g(x)^{-1}\) and apply the Product Rule (plus the Chain Rule, below, to differentiate \(g(x)^{-1}\)), and the familiar formula falls out on its own — which is also why the minus sign and the \(g^2\) in the denominator show up right where they do.
    🔗
    The Chain Rule is usually the rustiest one, and it’s the one we’ll lean on hardest all semester — substitution, our main integration technique in Chapter Chapter 3, is really the Chain Rule run backwards. Picture two gears meshed together: turn the first one, and it turns the second at a rate scaled by the gear ratio between them. A composed function \(f(g(x))\) works the same way — an "outer" function \(f\) wrapped around an "inner" function \(g\text{.}\) Nudging \(x\) nudges \(g(x)\) at rate \(g'(x)\text{,}\) and that nudge to \(g(x)\) in turn nudges \(f(g(x))\) at rate \(f'\) (evaluated at \(g(x)\text{,}\) not at \(x\)). The two rates multiply, gear-ratio style:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Chain Rule:} \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big[f(g(x))\big] = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x) \end{equation*}
    Alongside these rules for combining functions, you’ll need the rates of a handful of specific functions on recall. Two are worth a quick word rather than bare memorization. \(e^x\) is the unique function that is its own rate of change — at every instant it’s already growing exactly as fast as its current size demands, the way an account under continuous compounding grows faster the bigger it gets. And differentiating a wave produces another wave, a quarter-cycle out of step: the slope of \(\sin x\) traces out \(\cos x\text{,}\) whose own slope traces out \(-\sin x\text{,}\) and so on around.
    🔗

    Fact 1.1.9. Standard derivatives.

    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}[\sin x] &= \cos x & \frac{d}{dx}[\cos x] &= -\sin x \\ \frac{d}{dx}[\tan x] &= \sec^2 x & \frac{d}{dx}[e^x] &= e^x \\ \frac{d}{dx}[\ln x] &= \frac{1}{x} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Example 1.1.10. Product Rule and Chain Rule together.

    Differentiate \(f(x) = x^2 e^{3x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is a product of two functions, \(x^2\) and \(e^{3x}\text{,}\) so start with the Product Rule. The second factor needs the Chain Rule on its own: the "outside" function is \(e^{(\,\cdot\,)}\) and the "inside" function is \(3x\text{,}\) whose derivative is \(3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f'(x) &= \frac{d}{dx}[x^2]\cdot e^{3x} + x^2 \cdot \frac{d}{dx}[e^{3x}] \\ &= 2x\,e^{3x} + x^2 \cdot 3e^{3x} \\ &= e^{3x}(2x + 3x^2) \\ &= x e^{3x}(2 + 3x). \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.11. Quotient Rule.

    Differentiate \(g(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x - 3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} g'(x) &= \frac{(2x)(x-3) - (x^2+1)(1)}{(x-3)^2} \\ &= \frac{2x^2 - 6x - x^2 - 1}{(x-3)^2} \\ &= \frac{x^2 - 6x - 1}{(x-3)^2}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    For quick reference once the intuition above has done its job:
    🔗

    Fact 1.1.12. Basic differentiation rules, all in one place.

    For differentiable functions \(f\) and \(g\text{,}\) and a constant \(c\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \text{Constant Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[c] = 0 \\ \text{Power Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[x^n] = n x^{n-1} \\ \text{Constant Multiple Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[c \, f(x)] = c \, f'(x) \\ \text{Sum/Difference Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[f(x) \pm g(x)] = f'(x) \pm g'(x) \\ \text{Product Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)g(x)] = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x) \\ \text{Quotient Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}\!\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2} \\ \text{Chain Rule:} \qquad & \frac{d}{dx}\big[f(g(x))\big] = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x) \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.3 Critical points

    One more piece of vocabulary, and it follows straight from the tangent-line picture. Imagine hiking along the graph of \(f\) like a trail. At most points the ground under your feet is tilted — you’re climbing or descending. But right at the top of a hill, or the bottom of a valley, the ground goes momentarily flat: for that one instant, slope zero, before it tips the other way. That flatness is exactly \(f'(c) = 0\text{,}\) and it’s worth naming.
    🔗

    Definition 1.1.14. Critical point.

    A critical point of \(f\) is a value \(c\) in the domain of \(f\) where either \(f'(c) = 0\) (flat ground) or \(f'(c)\) does not exist (a sharp corner or break, where "the slope" isn’t even well-defined).
    🔗
    🔗
    Critical points are exactly the candidates for a hilltop or valley bottom — not every flat spot turns out to be one (a trail can flatten briefly and keep climbing, like a switchback), but every genuine peak or valley in the interior of the trail has to be a critical point first. That’s the entire engine behind optimization, which is where we’re headed next: find the flat spots, then work out which ones are actually the top or bottom.
    🔗

    Example 1.1.15. Finding critical points.

    Find the critical points of \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(f\) is a polynomial, so \(f'\) is defined everywhere; the only critical points come from \(f'(x) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f'(x) &= 3x^2 - 3 \\ 0 &= 3x^2 - 3 \\ 0 &= 3(x^2-1) \\ x &= \pm 1. \end{align*}
    So \(f\) has critical points at \(x=-1\) and \(x=1\text{.}\) (Sketch \(f(x)=x^3-3x\) if you want to see why: it has a local max at \(x=-1\) and a local min at \(x=1\text{,}\) exactly the two flat spots on that curve’s trail.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
