    Section 2.2 Optimization Problems

    Today’s Question.

    You have a fixed amount of something: fence, cardboard, money. How do you get the best possible outcome out of it?
    🔗
    🔗
    Best possible could mean the largest area. The largest volume. The lowest cost. The shortest distance.
    🔗
    Here is the good news. You already own the tool. In Subsection 1.1.3, the flat spots on a graph were the candidates for peaks and valleys. A peak is a maximum. A valley is a minimum. So “best possible” is really a derivative question.
    🔗
    The hard part is not the calculus. The hard part is getting from a paragraph of words to a function you can differentiate. So that is where we start.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.1 Word problems are translation problems

    A word problem is not asking you to guess a trick. It is asking you to translate.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{words} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{picture} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{equations} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{calculus} \end{equation*}
    So when you see one, do not start differentiating. Ask these questions first, in order:
    1. What am I trying to find? Look for words like largest, smallest, maximum, minimum, least, greatest.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What quantities are changing? Give them names and units.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. What is fixed? That usually gives you the constraint.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Can I draw a picture? Even a rough sketch helps a lot.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Can I write an equation for what I want? Something like \(A = xy\text{,}\) \(V = lwh\text{,}\) or \(P = 2l + 2w\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Can I rewrite it using just one variable?
      🔗
      🔗
    7. Which values actually make sense? That gives the feasible domain.
      🔗
      🔗
    8. Only now do we differentiate.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Rule of thumb.

    Do not differentiate a sentence. First turn the sentence into mathematics. Then differentiate.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.1. Warm-up: no calculus allowed.

    Just translate. Do not solve anything yet.
    1. A rectangle has perimeter \(60\) cm. Its length is \(x\) and its width is \(y\text{.}\) Write the constraint. Then write the quantity you would maximize to get the largest area.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. A farmer has \(300\) m of fencing for three sides of a rectangular field. A river forms the fourth side. The two sides touching the river are each \(x\) long, and the side across from the river is \(y\text{.}\) Write the constraint and the area.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Constraint: \(2x + 2y = 60\text{.}\) Maximize the area \(A = xy\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Constraint: \(2x + y = 300\) (only one \(y\text{,}\) because the river covers the other side). Area: \(A = xy\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.2 Two ingredients: objective and constraint

    Look back at the warm-up. Both problems had the same two pieces. Every optimization problem does.
    🔗

    Definition 2.2.2. Objective and constraint.

    The objective is the quantity we want to maximize or minimize.
    🔗
    The constraint is what limits our choices.
    🔗
    Optimization means finding the maximum or minimum value of the objective, subject to the constraint.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.3. Quick check.

    A rectangular garden uses exactly \(40\) meters of fencing. We want the biggest garden possible. Which one is the constraint?
    🔗
    • The perimeter: \(2x + 2y = 40\)
    • Right. The amount of fence is fixed, so it limits our choices.
    • The area: \(A = xy\)
    • That is what we want to make as big as possible. It is the objective, not the constraint.
    • The length: \(x = 10\)
    • Nobody told us the length. The length is one of the things we get to choose.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.3 The largest possible rectangle

    A farmer has \(40\) meters of fencing and wants a rectangular pen. (If you did the pre-assignment, you already met Pancake the alpaca. This is her pen.)
    🔗
    Before any calculus, make a prediction. Which will hold more: a long, skinny rectangle, or a more balanced one?
    🔗
    Now test your prediction. Drag the slider.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.5. What do you notice?

    Which width gave the most area? What shape was the pen?
    🔗
    Solution.
    A width of \(10\) m gives \(100\) square meters, the most of any width. The pen is a square. Skinny pens spend fence on length without gaining much area.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Why do derivatives help?

    Watch the area as you drag from left to right. It climbs, reaches a peak, then falls.
    🔗
    Climbing means \(A'(x) \gt 0\text{.}\) Falling means \(A'(x) \lt 0\text{.}\) Right at the top, it switches. So the peak sits where \(A'(x) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Dragging a slider found the answer this time. Calculus finds it every time, even when there is no slider.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.2.6. We do: prove the square wins.

    With \(40\) m of fence, find the dimensions of the rectangle with the largest area.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Objective: \(A = xy\text{.}\) Constraint: \(2x + 2y = 40\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1: Get down to one variable. Solve the constraint for \(y\text{:}\) \(y = 20 - x\text{.}\) Substitute:
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = x(20 - x) = 20x - x^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 2: Which values make sense? A side cannot be negative, so \(0 \le x \le 20\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3: Differentiate and find the critical point.
    \begin{align*} A'(x) &= 20 - 2x = 0 \\ x &= 10. \end{align*}
    Then \(y = 20 - 10 = 10\text{,}\) and \(A(10) = 100\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 4: Is it really a maximum? Test a number on each side. \(A'(5) = 10 \gt 0\text{,}\) so the area is increasing before \(x = 10\text{.}\) \(A'(15) = -10 \lt 0\text{,}\) so it is decreasing after. Increasing, then decreasing: a peak.
    🔗
    So the best pen is a \(10\) m by \(10\) m square, with area \(100\) square meters.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.4 The optimization routine

    That example had a shape. The same shape works every time.
    🔗

    The optimization routine.

    1. Draw and label a picture, when it helps.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Identify the objective.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Identify the constraint.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Rewrite the objective using one variable.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Find the feasible domain.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Differentiate and find the critical points.
      🔗
      🔗
    7. Decide which candidate gives the maximum or minimum.
      🔗
      🔗
    8. Answer the original question, with units.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Step 7 is the one people skip. Remember: a critical point is a candidate, not automatically an answer.
    🔗

    How do we know we found the best value?

    Two ways. Use whichever fits the problem.
    🔗
    First Derivative Test. Check the sign of \(f'\) on each side of the critical point. \(+\) then \(-\) means a maximum. \(-\) then \(+\) means a minimum. That is what we did for the pen.
    🔗
    Closed Interval Method. If the domain is a closed interval \([a, b]\text{,}\) plug the endpoints and every critical point into \(f\text{.}\) The biggest output is the maximum. The smallest is the minimum. For the pen: \(A(0) = 0\text{,}\) \(A(20) = 0\text{,}\) \(A(10) = 100\text{.}\) Same answer.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.2.7. We do: an open-top box.

    A piece of cardboard measures \(20\) inches by \(12\) inches. Cut a square of side \(x\) from each corner, then fold up the sides to make an open-top box. What value of \(x\) gives the largest volume?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Picture it. After folding, the height is the size of the cut: \(x\text{.}\) The long side lost \(x\) at both ends, so the length is \(20 - 2x\text{.}\) Same for the short side: the width is \(12 - 2x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Objective.
    \begin{equation*} V(x) = x(20 - 2x)(12 - 2x) = 4x^3 - 64x^2 + 240x. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    What makes sense? If \(x\) is tiny, the box is flat. If \(x\) is too big, there is no cardboard left. The width has to stay positive: \(12 - 2x \gt 0\text{,}\) so \(x \lt 6\text{.}\) The feasible domain is \(0 \lt x \lt 6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Differentiate.
    \begin{equation*} V'(x) = 12x^2 - 128x + 240 = 4(3x^2 - 32x + 60). \end{equation*}
    Set it equal to \(0\) and use the quadratic formula on \(3x^2 - 32x + 60 = 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} x = \frac{32 \pm \sqrt{32^2 - 4(3)(60)}}{2(3)} = \frac{32 \pm \sqrt{304}}{6}. \end{equation*}
    So \(x \approx 2.43\) or \(x \approx 8.24\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Which candidate? \(x \approx 8.24\) is outside the domain. You cannot cut \(8\)-inch squares from a \(12\)-inch side. That leaves \(x \approx 2.43\text{.}\) Check the signs: \(V'(1) = 124 \gt 0\) and \(V'(3) = -36 \lt 0\text{.}\) Increasing, then decreasing: a maximum.
    🔗
    Answer. Cut squares about \(2.43\) inches on a side. The box holds about \(262.7\) cubic inches.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.11. Conceptual check.

    1. Why do we use the constraint to eliminate a variable?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Why do we need the feasible domain?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Is every solution of \(f'(x) = 0\) a maximum or a minimum?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Our derivative tools work on functions of one variable. The constraint is how we trade two variables for one.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The algebra does not know the problem is about cardboard. It happily hands you answers like \(x \approx 8.24\) that cannot happen. The domain throws those out.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. No. It is only a candidate. You still have to check, with the First Derivative Test or the Closed Interval Method.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.12. Exit ticket.

    1. In one sentence, what is an optimization problem?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What is the difference between an objective and a constraint?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. What should you do before differentiating a word problem?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Finding the best possible value of some quantity, like the largest area or the lowest cost, when something limits your choices.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The objective is what you want to make as big or as small as possible. The constraint is what is fixed.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Translate: name the variables, draw a picture, write the objective and the constraint, and get down to one variable.
      🔗
      🔗

    Big picture.

    Optimization is mostly modeling first and calculus second.
    🔗
    The derivative finds the spot where a quantity stops getting better and starts getting worse.
