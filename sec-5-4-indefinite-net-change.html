    Section 4.4 Indefinite Integrals and the Net Change Theorem

    Today’s Question.

    When do we want a function, and when do we want an accumulated amount?
    🔗
    🔗
    Consider the rate \(f(x) = 3x^2\text{.}\) Here are two questions that sound similar but are not the same question at all:
    🔗
    Question A: find all functions whose derivative is \(3x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Question B: find the accumulated amount from \(x=0\) to \(x=2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Question A wants a function — really, a whole family of them. Question B wants a single number. We already answered Question B in Section 4.3: \(\int_0^2 3x^2\,dx = 8\text{.}\) Today we give Question A its own notation, and get precise about why these two questions need two different tools.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.4.1 Indefinite integral: find the family

    Suppose \(F'(x) = f(x)\text{,}\) so \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\text{.}\) We now give the family of all antiderivatives its own symbol, dropping the bounds entirely:
    🔗

    Definition 4.4.1. Indefinite integral.

    \begin{equation*} \int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C, \end{equation*}
    where \(F' = f\) and \(C\) is an arbitrary constant. This is called an indefinite integral.
    🔗
    🔗
    For example, \(\displaystyle\int 3x^2\,dx = x^3+C\text{.}\) Why the \(+C\text{?}\) Because \(\frac{d}{dx}(x^3) = 3x^2\text{,}\) but so does \(\frac{d}{dx}(x^3+7)\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}(x^3-100)\text{.}\) Every one of these is an equally correct answer to “find a function whose derivative is \(3x^2\text{,}\)” so the indefinite integral represents all of them at once: an entire family of antiderivatives.
    🔗

    Indefinite integral, at a glance.

    \(\displaystyle\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C\text{.}\) No bounds. The answer is a family of functions. Always include \(+C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.4.2 Side by side with the definite integral

    Compare this new symbol to the one from Section 4.2 and Section 4.3:
    🔗
    Indefinite integral \(\int f(x)\,dx\) Definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\)
    no bounds has bounds
    answer is a family of functions answer is a number
    include \(+C\) never include \(+C\)
    recover a quantity from its rate find net accumulation over an interval
    One question settles which tool to reach for:
    🔗

    The question to ask.

    Do I need a function, or do I need the total change over an interval?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.4.2. Which one should I use?

    For each situation, is the right tool an indefinite integral or a definite integral?
    1. The velocity of a car is known. Find a formula for its position.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Water flows into a tank at rate \(r(t)\text{.}\) Find how much water enters between \(t=2\) and \(t=7\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The marginal cost is \(C'(x)\text{.}\) Find the change in cost from producing 100 items to 150 items.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Indefinite integral — “a formula” asks for a function.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Definite integral — “between \(t=2\) and \(t=7\)” asks for net change over an interval.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Definite integral — “the change from…to…” asks for net change over an interval.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.4.3 Indefinite integral toolkit

    Finding an indefinite integral means reversing a derivative rule you already know.
    🔗
    Derivative rule Integral rule
    \(\frac{d}{dx}(x^{n+1}) = (n+1)x^n\) \(\int x^n\,dx = \dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C,\ n\neq-1\)
    \(\frac{d}{dx}(\ln|x|) = \frac1x\) \(\int \frac1x\,dx = \ln|x|+C\)
    \(\frac{d}{dx}(e^x) = e^x\) \(\int e^x\,dx = e^x+C\)
    \(\frac{d}{dx}(a^x) = a^x\ln a\) \(\int a^x\,dx = \dfrac{a^x}{\ln a}+C\)
    \(\frac{d}{dx}(-\cos x) = \sin x\) \(\int \sin x\,dx = -\cos x+C\)
    \(\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x\) \(\int \cos x\,dx = \sin x+C\)
    \(\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \sec^2x\) \(\int \sec^2x\,dx = \tan x+C\)
    None of these need to be memorized as isolated facts — every single one can be checked by differentiating the right-hand side and confirming it matches the left.
    🔗
    Roots and fractions are usually easier to integrate once they are rewritten with exponents, exactly as in derivative work. For example,
    \begin{equation*} \int \left(\sqrt{x}+\frac{2}{x^3}\right)dx = \int \left(x^{1/2}+2x^{-3}\right)dx = \frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}-\frac{1}{x^2}+C. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Rewrite first, integrate second.

    One exception never to forget: \(\displaystyle\int \frac1x\,dx = \ln|x|+C\text{.}\) The power rule for integration explicitly excludes \(n=-1\) — it would require dividing by \(0\text{.}\) That special case is exactly where the logarithm rule takes over.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.4.3. You do: algebraic integrals.

    1. Find \(\displaystyle\int (6x^5-4x^2+7)\,dx\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find \(\displaystyle\int \left(\frac{2}{x}+x^4\right)dx\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(\displaystyle x^6-\tfrac43x^3+7x+C\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle 2\ln|x|+\tfrac15x^5+C\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    To check any indefinite integral, differentiate your answer and make sure it reproduces the original integrand — the same habit of checking antiderivatives from Section 3.3.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.4.4 From rate to quantity: one rate, two questions

    Suppose a car has velocity \(v(t) = 6t-4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Question A: find the position function. Since \(s'(t) = v(t)\text{,}\) we need an indefinite integral:
    \begin{equation*} s(t) = \int (6t-4)\,dt = 3t^2-4t+C. \end{equation*}
    If we also know \(s(0)=10\text{,}\) then \(10=C\text{,}\) pinning down one member of the family: \(s(t) = 3t^2-4t+10\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Question B: find the displacement from \(t=1\) to \(t=4\text{.}\) Now we need a definite integral:
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^4 (6t-4)\,dt = \bigl[3t^2-4t\bigr]_1^4 = (48-16)-(3-4) = 32-(-1) = 33. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.4.4. Same rate, different question.

    🔗

    Same rate function, different question.

    Need the quantity as a function? Indefinite integral (plus an initial condition, if you have one). Need the change over an interval? Definite integral.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.4.5 The net change theorem

    Let \(Q(t)\) be any quantity and \(Q'(t)\) its rate of change. FTC Part 2, applied to \(Q\) instead of a generic \(f\text{,}\) says
    🔗

    Definition 4.4.5. Net change theorem.

    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b Q'(t)\,dt = Q(b) - Q(a). \end{equation*}
    In words: the integral of the rate equals the net change in the quantity. Equivalently,
    \begin{equation*} Q(b) = Q(a) + \int_a^b Q'(t)\,dt, \end{equation*}
    or: final amount equals initial amount plus net change.
    🔗
    🔗
    This is nothing new mathematically — it is FTC Part 2 again — but the “final \(=\) initial \(+\) change” framing is exactly how these integrals show up in applications.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.4.6 Applications: final = initial + net change

    A tank initially contains \(500\) liters of water. Water enters at the rate \(r(t) = 20-2t\) liters per minute for \(0 \le t \le 5\text{.}\) This is exactly the setup behind the Doenet pre-assignment on Pancake’s water trough.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.4.6. Water in a tank.

    🔗
    Marginal cost gives another standard use of the net change theorem. If the marginal cost of producing \(x\) items is \(C'(x) = 0.02x+5\) dollars per item, the increase in cost from 100 to 150 items is
    \begin{equation*} C(150)-C(100) = \int_{100}^{150}(0.02x+5)\,dx = \bigl[0.01x^2+5x\bigr]_{100}^{150} = 975-600 = 375 \text{ dollars}. \end{equation*}
    The phrase “increase in cost” is the giveaway: it is asking for a net change over an interval, so it needs a definite integral, not a cost function.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.4.7 Net change is not the same as total distance

    Suppose velocity is \(v(t) = t-2\) for \(0 \le t \le 4\text{.}\) The particle moves backward for \(0 < t < 2\) and forward for \(2 < t < 4\text{.}\) The net displacement is
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^4 (t-2)\,dt = \left[\frac{t^2}{2}-2t\right]_0^4 = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.4.7. Zero displacement — did the particle not move?

    The displacement above came out to \(0\text{.}\) Does that mean the particle never moved?
    🔗
    • Yes — zero displacement means the particle stayed still the whole time.
    • Not quite — the particle moved backward, then forward by the same amount, so the net change cancels even though real distance was covered.
    • No — the backward motion and the forward motion are the same size and cancel in the net change, even though the particle traveled a positive distance.
    • Right. The word “net” in the Net Change Theorem matters: negative velocity subtracts from the total.
    🔗
    To find the actual distance traveled, every bit of motion has to count positively:
    \begin{equation*} \text{total distance} = \int_a^b |v(t)|\,dt, \end{equation*}
    which in practice means splitting the integral wherever \(v(t)\) changes sign and taking absolute values on each piece. For this example, that gives a total distance of \(4\text{,}\) not \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.4.8 Putting it together

    Checkpoint 4.4.8. Conceptual check.

    1. What is the main difference between \(\int f(x)\,dx\) and \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Why does an indefinite integral need \(+C\text{,}\) while a definite integral does not?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If \(Q'(t)\) is measured in liters per minute, what are the units of \(\int_2^8 Q'(t)\,dt\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. The indefinite integral is a family of antiderivative functions; the definite integral is a single number, the net accumulation from \(a\) to \(b\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Differentiation loses constant information, so every antiderivative needs \(+C\) to represent the whole family; in a definite integral the constant appears in both \(F(b)+C\) and \(F(a)+C\) and cancels.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Liters — (liters per minute) \(\times\) minutes \(=\) liters.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.4.9. Exit ticket.

    🔗

    Today’s main idea.

    If you want to recover a function from its rate, use an indefinite integral: \(\int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+C\text{.}\) If you want the net accumulated change over an interval, use a definite integral: \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)\text{.}\) For applications, remember final amount \(=\) initial amount \(+\) net change.
    🔗
    Every antiderivative rule we have used so far has reversed a derivative we already knew directly — power rule, trig rule, exponential rule. But what happens when the rate in front of us does not match any rule we know, until we notice it is secretly a chain rule in disguise? That puzzle is the Substitution Rule.
