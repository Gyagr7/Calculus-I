<title>Pre-Assignment: Pancake's Substitution Puzzle</title>

<p>
  Pancake is training for the fall race, and her coach hands her a new
  rate to integrate before Thursday:
</p>

<p>
  <m>\displaystyle\int 2x(x^2+1)^3\,dx</m>
</p>

<p>
  She reaches for her toolkit from last class: power rule, exponential
  rule, trig rule. None of them match directly. The integrand is not a
  single power of <m>x</m> -- it is a whole expression, <m>(x^2+1)</m>,
  raised to a power, multiplied by something else out front.
</p>

<p>
  <alert>Question 1.</alert> What do you think is going on here?
</p>

<answer>
  <label>What's going on with this integral?</label>
  <choice credit="0">You can expand <m>(x^2+1)^3</m> with the binomial theorem, then the power rule handles each term -- there is no shortcut here.</choice>
  <choice credit="1"><m>2x</m> looks suspicious. It is exactly the derivative of <m>x^2+1</m>, the expression being cubed. Maybe that is not a coincidence.</choice>
  <choice credit="0">This cannot be integrated at all with the tools from this course.</choice>
</answer>

<p>
  You are right to be suspicious. Expanding <m>(x^2+1)^3</m> and
  multiplying through by <m>2x</m> would work eventually, but it is slow
  and easy to mess up. There is a much faster path -- if you notice the
  right pattern.
</p>

<p>
  Here is the pattern: somewhere inside this integral, one piece is an
  <em>inside function</em>, and another piece is exactly its own
  derivative, just riding along as a factor. When that happens, calculus
  lets you rename the whole inside function as one new letter, <m>u</m>,
  and the messy integral in <m>x</m> turns into a simple integral in
  <m>u</m>.
</p>

<p>
  <alert>Question 2.</alert> In <m>\displaystyle\int 2x(x^2+1)^3\,dx</m>,
  let <m>u = x^2+1</m>. Which of these is <m>du</m>?
</p>

<answer>
  <label>Which expression is du?</label>
  <choice credit="1"><m>2x\,dx</m></choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>x^2\,dx</m></choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>2x</m></choice>
</answer>

<p>
  Now the entire integral has a new face. Every <m>x^2+1</m> becomes
  <m>u</m>, and the leftover <m>2x\,dx</m> is exactly <m>du</m> -- it
  disappears into the substitution, not into the answer.
</p>

<p>
  <alert>Question 3.</alert> Rewrite <m>\displaystyle\int 2x(x^2+1)^3\,dx</m>
  entirely in terms of <m>u</m>.
</p>

<answer>
  <label>The integral rewritten in u</label>
  <choice credit="1"><m>\displaystyle\int u^3\,du</m></choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>\displaystyle\int u^3\,dx</m></choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>\displaystyle\int 2x\,u^3\,du</m></choice>
</answer>

<p>
  That is a power-rule integral you already know how to do.
</p>

<p>
  <alert>Question 4.</alert> Evaluate <m>\displaystyle\int u^3\,du</m>,
  then substitute back <m>u = x^2+1</m> to answer in terms of <m>x</m>.
  What is <m>\displaystyle\int 2x(x^2+1)^3\,dx</m>?
</p>

<answer>
  <label>The antiderivative, back in terms of x</label>
  <choice credit="1"><m>\dfrac{(x^2+1)^4}{4} + C</m></choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>(x^2+1)^4 + C</m></choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>4(x^2+1)^3 + C</m></choice>
</answer>

<p>
  Check it the way you always check an indefinite integral: differentiate
  your answer. If the substitution was done correctly,
  <m>\frac{d}{dx}\left[\dfrac{(x^2+1)^4}{4}\right] = (x^2+1)^3 \cdot 2x</m>
  by the chain rule -- exactly the original integrand. Substitution and
  the chain rule are the same move, run in opposite directions.
</p>

<p>
  <alert>Question 5.</alert> Pancake's coach hands her a second rate
  before the race: <m>\displaystyle\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx</m>. What
  substitution <m>u</m> turns this into a trig-rule integral you already
  know?
</p>

<answer>
  <label>Choose the substitution</label>
  <choice credit="1"><m>u = x^2</m>, since <m>du = 2x\,dx</m> is exactly the leftover factor.</choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>u = \cos(x^2)</m>, since that is the most complicated piece.</choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>u = 2x</m>, since that is the piece sitting out front.</choice>
</answer>

<p>
  <alert>Question 6.</alert> <m>\displaystyle\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx =</m>
</p>

<answer>
  <label>The antiderivative of 2x cos(x^2)</label>
  <choice credit="1"><m>\sin(x^2) + C</m></choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>\cos(x^2) + C</m></choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>-\cos(x^2) + C</m></choice>
</answer>

<p>
  Here is the slider version of the same idea. Drag <m>x</m> and watch two
  numbers at once: the inside function <m>u(x) = x^2</m>, and its rate of
  change <m>u'(x) = 2x</m>. The factor sitting out front of every
  substitution problem in this activity is never random -- it is always
  <m>u'(x)</m>, caught in the act.
</p>

<slider name="xSlide" from="0" to="3" step="0.25" initialValue="1"/>

  <m>x =</m> <number>$xSlide</number>,
  &#160;&#160;
  <m>u(x) = x^2 =</m> <number>($xSlide)^2</number>,
  &#160;&#160;
  <m>u'(x) = 2x =</m> <number>2$xSlide</number>
</p>

<p>
  Every time you see a factor out front that matches the derivative of
  something more complicated behind it, that factor is <m>du</m> in
  disguise. Finding it is the whole puzzle.
</p>

<p>
  <alert>Question 7 (exit ticket).</alert>
  One more for the road: <m>\displaystyle\int 3x^2\sin(x^3)\,dx</m>.
  First, identify <m>u</m>.
</p>

<answer>
  <label>Identify u</label>
  <award>x^3</award>
</answer>

<p>
  <alert>Question 8 (exit ticket).</alert>
  Now give the full antiderivative: <m>\displaystyle\int 3x^2\sin(x^3)\,dx =</m>
</p>

<answer>
  <label>The full antiderivative</label>
  <choice credit="1"><m>-\cos(x^3) + C</m></choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>\cos(x^3) + C</m></choice>
  <choice credit="0"><m>-3x^2\cos(x^3) + C</m></choice>
</answer>

<p>
  Not every antiderivative hands itself to you the way <m>\int x^n\,dx</m>
  does. Sometimes the function in front of you is really two functions
  tangled together by the chain rule, and finding the antiderivative
  means untangling them first. That untangling is the Substitution Rule
  -- see you in class to make it official.
</p>
