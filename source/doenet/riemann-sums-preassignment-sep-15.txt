<title>Pre-Assignment: Guess How Far Pancake Ran</title>

<p>
  Pancake the alpaca is running again. (She still cannot race for real
  &#8212; long story.) This time we timed her speed, not her position. At
  time <m>t</m> seconds, her speed is <m>v(t) = 3t^2</m> meters per
  second. We checked her speed every half second.
</p>

<p>
  Here are the speeds we recorded, from <m>t = 0</m> to <m>t = 2</m>
  seconds:
</p>

<p>
  <m>t = 0</m>: <m>v = 0</m> m/s &#160;&#160;|&#160;&#160;
  <m>t = 0.5</m>: <m>v = 0.75</m> m/s &#160;&#160;|&#160;&#160;
  <m>t = 1</m>: <m>v = 3</m> m/s &#160;&#160;|&#160;&#160;
  <m>t = 1.5</m>: <m>v = 6.75</m> m/s &#160;&#160;|&#160;&#160;
  <m>t = 2</m>: <m>v = 12</m> m/s
</p>

<p>
  Your job: use this table to guess how far Pancake ran. This takes about
  10 minutes. A wrong guess is okay. That is how estimates work.
</p>

<p>
  <alert>Question 1.</alert> Pretend Pancake's speed did not change
  during each half-second. Use the speed from the <em>start</em> of each
  half-second: <m>0</m>, then <m>0.75</m>, then <m>3</m>, then <m>6.75</m>
  meters per second. Multiply each speed by <m>0.5</m> seconds. Then add
  up all four distances. What is the total, in meters?
</p>

<answer>
  <label>Distance estimate using the start-of-interval speeds</label>
  <award>5.25</award>
</answer>

<p>
  This method has a name: the <em>left-endpoint estimate</em>. Picture
  four thin rectangles under the speed curve. Each rectangle's height is
  the speed at the left edge of that part. The first rectangle has height
  <m>0</m>. So you cannot see it in the picture.
</p>

<graph xmin="-0.2" xmax="2.3" ymin="-1" ymax="13">

  <function>3x^2</function>

<p>
  <alert>Question 2.</alert> Now try the opposite method. Use the speed
  from the <em>end</em> of each half-second: <m>0.75</m>, then <m>3</m>,
  then <m>6.75</m>, then <m>12</m> meters per second. Multiply each speed
  by <m>0.5</m> seconds. Add up all four distances. What is the total, in
  meters?
</p>

<answer>
  <label>Distance estimate using the end-of-interval speeds</label>
  <award>11.25</award>
</answer>

<p>
  This method also has a name: the <em>right-endpoint estimate</em>. It
  works the same way. But now each rectangle's height matches the speed
  at the <em>right</em> edge.
</p>

<p>
  <alert>Question 3.</alert> Pancake's speed only went up. It never
  slowed down. Because of this, what do you know for sure about your two
  estimates?
</p>

<answer>
  <label>Which estimate is which, for a speeding-up run?</label>
  <choice credit="1">The left estimate is too low. The right estimate is too high.</choice>
  <choice credit="0">The left estimate is too high. The right estimate is too low.</choice>
  <choice credit="0">Both estimates are too low.</choice>
  <choice credit="0">Both estimates are too high.</choice>
</answer>

<p>
  Here is the real answer: Pancake ran exactly <m>8</m> meters. You may
  remember this number <m>8</m> from the antiderivatives section. Look
  at your two guesses: <m>5.25</m> and <m>11.25</m>. The real answer,
  <m>8</m>, sits between them.
</p>

<p>
  <alert>Question 4.</alert> Now imagine using <m>40</m> rectangles
  instead of <m>4</m>. Each rectangle would be much thinner. What do you
  think happens to the gap between your left estimate and your right
  estimate?
</p>

<answer>
  <label>Your conjecture about more, thinner rectangles</label>
  <choice credit="1">The gap gets smaller. Both estimates get closer to the true answer.</choice>
  <choice credit="0">The gap stays the same size, no matter how many rectangles you use.</choice>
  <choice credit="0">The gap gets bigger as you use more rectangles.</choice>
</answer>

<p>
  Remember your guess. On Tuesday, we will make "more, thinner
  rectangles" exact. This idea becomes the definition of the definite
  integral.
</p>
